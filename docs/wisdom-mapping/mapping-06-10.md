# Wisdom-guidance mapping — categories 6–10

**Scope.** This file maps real, sourced content across three pillars — Eastern philosophy, shadow work, and evidence-based self-directed psychology methodology — for the five v1.0 taxonomy categories assigned to this pass: 6 (Shame), 7 (Rejection/heartbreak), 8 (Feeling stuck/can't decide), 9 (Fear of failure), 10 (Substance use). It complements, and does not duplicate, the clinical grounding in [`CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`](../CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md). Per the app's core constraint, nothing below is LLM-invented: every claim traces to a real text, a real named teacher/author's documented work, or a real peer-reviewed study, cited inline and listed again in Sources at the end. Where an exact quotation could not be verified, the teaching is described and the describing source is cited instead of putting invented words in quotation marks.

Root mappings referenced below are as established in [`TAXONOMY_V1.0.md`](../TAXONOMY_V1.0.md).

## 6. Shame / feeling fundamentally not okay
**Roots: Meaninglessness, Isolation**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
Tibetan Buddhist teacher Pema Chödrön teaches *maitri* (Sanskrit/Tibetan for loving-kindness, "unconditional friendliness with oneself") in her book *When Things Fall Apart: Heart Advice for Difficult Times* (Shambhala, 1996) as the direct antidote to the self-aversion and self-hatred that make shame feel unbearable. The practice is explicitly framed as training in sitting with oneself "as we are," without the "good/bad" labeling that shame runs on — for people who find it far easier to extend loving-kindness to others than to themselves. This speaks directly to Isolation (shame's core move is separating the self from a shared human "we're all okay" ground) and to Meaninglessness (shame frames the self as fundamentally flawed rather than merely as having done something wrong).

### B. Shadow work
Carl Jung located shame's psychological engine in the shadow: "Everyone carries a shadow, and the less it is embodied in the individual's conscious life, the blacker and denser it is" (*Psychology and Religion: West and East*, Collected Works Vol. 11, 1938/1958, p. 131). Building on this, Jungian analysts Connie Zweig and Steve Wolf, in *Romancing the Shadow: Illuminating the Dark Side of the Soul* (Ballantine, 1997), describe the shadow specifically as the repository of what a person's family or culture found "forbidden, shamed, or taboo" in them growing up — meaning shame is not incidental to shadow formation but literally the mechanism that exiles a trait into the shadow in the first place. Shadow work here means locating which parts of the self were shamed into hiding, not just managing the resulting shame feeling.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Kristin Neff's self-compassion research (Neff, K.D., "Self-Compassion: An Alternative Conceptualization of a Healthy Attitude Toward Oneself," *Self and Identity*, 2(2), 2003, 85–101) defines self-compassion as three components — self-kindness vs. self-judgment, common humanity vs. isolation, and mindfulness vs. over-identification — that map almost one-to-one onto shame's structure (harsh self-judgment, isolating "I'm the only one," and being swallowed by the feeling). This is a distinct, non-clinical, self-administrable framework (the validated Self-Compassion Scale) separate from clinical shame-treatment approaches.

### D. Synthesis note
Maitri gives a contemplative practice for meeting shame without the self-attack; Zweig/Wolf and Jung explain *why* the shamed material got buried in the first place, giving the person a way to understand rather than just soothe the feeling; Neff's research operationalizes the same move (self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness) into something measurable and practicable outside a clinical setting. Carried forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`: cross-cultural research shows collectivist/Eastern shame is often externalized/face-based rather than the internalized-individual-flaw model this category's Western clinical grounding (Shame Resilience Theory) assumes — so Eastern source material for this category should be selected for its address to the internal experience of "not-okay-ness" (as Chödrön's teaching is), not force-fit into a face/honor framing it wasn't built for.

## 7. Rejection / heartbreak
**Roots: Isolation, Meaninglessness**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The *Sallatha Sutta* ("The Arrow," Saṃyutta Nikāya 36.6), a Theravada Buddhist discourse, teaches the "two arrows": the first arrow is the pain of the loss itself (unavoidable), the second arrow is the added mental suffering — sorrow, lamentation, self-recrimination — that an "uninstructed" person shoots at themselves on top of it, which is avoidable. The sutta states that a well-trained practitioner "feels one pain: physical [i.e., the original loss], but not mental" (accesstoinsight.org translation by Thanissaro Bhikkhu). Applied to heartbreak, this distinguishes the reality of the loss from the additional layer of story ("I'm unlovable," "I'll always be alone") that compounds it — directly addressing both Isolation and Meaninglessness without denying the loss is real.

### B. Shadow work
Jungian analyst James Hollis, in *The Eden Project: In Search of the Magical Other* (Inner City Books, 1998), argues that much of the devastation of romantic rejection comes from the collapse of the "magical Other" fantasy — the unconscious hope that another person will complete us, rescue us from our own unlived life, and relieve us of the work of finding our own wholeness. Hollis frames heartbreak's intensity as partly grief for the relationship and partly a shadow confrontation: the loss forces a person back onto the discovery that "the magical Other does not, and cannot, exist," and that the search for wholeness has to include the parts of the self that were being outsourced to the partner.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Sbarra, D.A., Smith, H.L., & Mehl, M.R., "When Leaving Your Ex, Love Yourself: Observational Ratings of Self-Compassion Predict the Course of Emotional Recovery Following Marital Separation," *Psychological Science*, 23(3), 2012, 261–269. In this study of 109 recently separated/divorced adults, independent raters' scores of participants' self-compassion (self-kindness, common humanity, emotional equanimity) in a brief recorded narrative predicted less intrusive distress at the outset and continued to predict better emotional recovery up to 9 months later — a direct, breakup-specific evidence base for a self-compassion practice (distinct from the general self-compassion research cited for category 6).

### D. Synthesis note
The second-arrow teaching separates the unavoidable pain of loss from the avoidable self-story that compounds it; Hollis's magical-Other concept explains why breakups can feel existentially catastrophic rather than merely sad (a fantasy of completion is dying, not just a relationship); the Sbarra et al. findings give an evidence base for responding to that with self-compassion specifically after a breakup. Carried forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`: this category may be clinically under-weighted by default — a 2022-reported study (n=2,022) found breakup-related PTSD-checklist scores exceeded DSM-5 Criterion A trauma-event scores in 72.9% of cases — so content shouldn't assume mild distress; presentations with flashbacks, dissociation, or functional collapse should be flagged toward referral rather than philosophy-only content. Also carried forward: the taxonomy's dividing line from Betrayal (#12) is that the relationship *ended* here, vs. trust *violated* with the relationship possibly continuing there.

## 8. Feeling stuck / can't decide
**Root: Freedom**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The *Tao Te Ching*, attributed to Laozi, chapter 15 (Stephen Mitchell translation, HarperPerennial, 1988) asks: "Do you have the patience to wait till your mud settles and the water is clear? Can you remain unmoving till the right action arises by itself?" This is a Taoist teaching on *wu wei* (non-forcing, effortless right action) applied directly to indecision: it reframes "stuck" not as a failure to act but as a state that, tolerated with patience rather than forced, resolves on its own — a direct address to the Freedom root's demand that one choose without absolute ground for the choice, by questioning whether the choice must be forced at all.

### B. Shadow work
Jungian analyst Marie-Louise von Franz, in *The Problem of the Puer Aeternus* (Inner City Books, 1970/2000), describes the "provisional life": a psychological stance in which a person treats their current job, relationship, or path as not-yet-real, a placeholder until "real life" begins — and, because committing to any one path means giving up the others, stays unable to choose, keeping every option notionally open while fully entering none. Von Franz frames this "puer" (eternal-youth) pattern as a shadow issue: the avoided material isn't the decision itself but the deeper fear of the limitation and loss that any real commitment requires.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Acceptance and Commitment Therapy's values-clarification and committed-action processes (Hayes, S.C., Strosahl, K.D., & Wilson, K.G., *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: The Process and Practice of Mindful Change*, 2nd ed., Guilford Press, 2012) offer a non-clinical, self-directed practice: rather than waiting for certainty about which choice is "right," a person identifies what they value in the relevant life domain and takes a committed step in that direction, treating the discomfort of not-knowing as something to carry alongside the action rather than resolve before acting.

### D. Synthesis note
The Tao Te Ching teaching questions whether forcing a decision is even the right frame; von Franz's provisional-life concept names the shadow fear (of loss/limitation) that keeps someone stuck rather than treating it as a simple information problem; ACT's values-and-committed-action process gives a concrete practice for acting without first resolving all uncertainty. Carried forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`: this is flagged as the taxonomy's cleanest single-root match (Freedom only, well-established), but "ACT helps with chronic indecision" specifically is noted there as a reasonable extrapolation from ACT's general mechanism, not a targeted clinical-trial finding for indecision — that gap is preserved here rather than overstated.

## 9. Fear of failure
**Roots: Freedom, Death**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The *Bhagavad Gita*, chapter 2, verse 47, teaches *nishkama karma* (action without attachment to outcome): "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, and never be attached to not doing your duty" (a standard rendering; cf. Swami Mukundananda's and Prabhupada's translations at holy-bhagavad-gita.org and vedabase.io). Krishna's counsel to Arjuna is precisely about acting fully while releasing the outcome — success or failure — from one's identity or self-worth, which speaks directly to fear of failure's Freedom-root mechanism (the demand to act and choose despite no guarantee of the result).

### B. Shadow work
Jungian analyst Robert A. Johnson, in *Owning Your Own Shadow: Understanding the Dark Side of the Psyche* (HarperOne, 1991), describes the "golden shadow" — disowned brilliance, competence, and potential that a person buries (often because early environments punished or didn't make room for it) and then unconsciously projects onto admired others, worshipping in them what they can't own in themselves. Applied to fear of failure, this reframes the fear as sometimes protecting against the risk of *discovering* one's own capability and having to live up to it, not only against the risk of falling short.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Carol Dweck's growth-mindset research (*Mindset: The New Psychology of Success*, Random House, 2006, building on her peer-reviewed program of research at Stanford) found that people holding a "fixed mindset" (abilities are static) dread failure as a verdict on their basic worth, avoiding challenge as a result, while people holding a "growth mindset" (abilities develop through effort) treat failure as information that supports learning rather than as identity-threatening. This is a well-established, non-clinical, self-directed framework distinct from clinical treatment of failure-related anxiety.

### D. Synthesis note
The Gita's teaching separates right action from attachment to its result, directly loosening failure's grip on identity; Johnson's golden-shadow concept adds a psychologically specific reason fear of failure can persist even when someone is clearly capable; Dweck's research gives an evidence-based, practicable reframe of failure as data rather than verdict. Carried forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`: the Death-root connection for this category runs through Terror Management Theory/ego-threat, not literal mortality, and a well-powered 2023 replication attempt found TMT's core mortality-salience effect near zero — so this category's Death-root material should lean on impermanence/non-attachment-to-outcome themes (as the Gita citation above does), not death imagery, consistent with the matching-layer note already recorded in `TAXONOMY_V1.0.md`.

## 10. Substance use
**Roots: Death, Meaninglessness**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The fifth of the Buddhist Five Precepts (pañca-sīla), recited across Theravada practice, is to abstain from *surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhānā* — intoxicants that are "the basis of heedlessness" (surā/meraya = intoxicating liquor, majja = intoxicant, pamāda = heedlessness/negligence) (accesstoinsight.org). Thich Nhat Hanh's modern restatement of this precept as the Fifth Mindfulness Training, "Nourishment and Healing," in the Plum Village tradition (see plumvillage.org/mindfulness/the-5-mindfulness-trainings), broadens it from a prohibition into a practice of mindful consumption — attending with awareness to what one takes in (substances, but also food, media, company) in service of the same underlying aim: not using consumption to avoid or numb present-moment experience.

### B. Shadow work
Carl Jung's January 1961 letter to Bill Wilson, co-founder of Alcoholics Anonymous — written regarding Jung's earlier treatment of Wilson's acquaintance Roland H. — contains Jung's formulation *spiritus contra spiritum* ("spirit against spirit"): that the alcoholic's craving is, at a low level, the same spiritual thirst for wholeness that mystical or religious experience answers at a high level, and that the Latin word *spiritus* names both the alcoholic drink and the highest religious experience. This documented, historically influential letter (widely reproduced, e.g. by the AA archives community and discussed at wccm.org and aaforagnostics.com) frames addiction as a compensatory reach for something real (wholeness, meaning, transcendence) via the wrong means — directly relevant to shadow work's view of compulsive/addictive behavior as displaced pursuit of disowned or unmet needs, rather than simple moral failure.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Krentzman, A.R., "Review of the Application of Positive Psychology to Substance Use, Addiction, and Recovery Research," *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors*, 27(1), 2013, 151–165, surveys the (then-nascent, now-growing — see the 2025 update by Carlon et al. in *Addiction*) evidence base for positive-psychology interventions — gratitude practice, identification of personal strengths, savoring, acts of kindness — as adjuncts that increase positive affect and support recovery, distinct from clinical/medical addiction treatment. This is offered here as the non-clinical, self-directed pillar; it is explicitly a *complement* to treatment, not a replacement, consistent with the safety note below.

### D. Synthesis note — safety note carried forward, read this before using this category's content
The Fifth Precept/Fifth Mindfulness Training frame substance use as an attention/consumption practice; Jung's letter to Wilson reframes the craving itself as a distorted reach for real meaning or wholeness (Meaninglessness root); Krentzman's review gives a non-clinical positive-psychology complement consistent with that reframe (Death root, via the mortality/hopelessness angle documented in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`, not an impermanence-appreciation angle). **Explicit safety carry-forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`: Substance use (#10) has a hard ceiling.** Real overdose/withdrawal mortality risk exists (alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be fatal), so this category cannot be philosophy-content-only. The three pillars above are appropriate **only** for a person who is not in active withdrawal or crisis — they address the affect-regulation/meaning dimension of substance use (Khantzian's self-medication hypothesis and Frankl's existential-vacuum concept are the clinical-side anchors named in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md` for this same angle). Wisdom/philosophy content here must never substitute for the crisis-resource redirect or medical care whenever overdose or withdrawal risk is present — that redirect takes absolute precedence, per the taxonomy's existing hard exclusion of acute crisis presentations from philosophy-matching scope.
