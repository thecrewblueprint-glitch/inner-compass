# Wisdom mapping — categories 21–25 (v1.0, final batch)

**Scope.** Per-category mapping of real, sourced content across three pillars — (A) Eastern
philosophy/metaphysical teaching, (B) Jungian/post-Jungian shadow work, (C) evidence-based,
non-clinical self-directed psychology practice — for the last five [`TAXONOMY_V1.0.md`](../TAXONOMY_V1.0.md)
categories, plus a synthesis note per category. Built on the existential-root mappings in
that document and the safety findings in [`CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`](../CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md) and
[`research/kb-21-25.md`](../research/kb-21-25.md). Categories 1–20 are out of scope and were
not touched; no other files were modified.

**A note on sourcing standard.** Every claim below traces to a real text, a real named
teacher/researcher with a real documented work, or a real study/review. Where a quote is
reproduced, it is drawn from a cited translation or a source page that itself attributes the
words to the named teacher — nothing here was invented or reconstructed from memory alone.
Where a search did not turn up a source precise enough to cite with confidence (a specific
verse number, a specific study), that gap is stated explicitly rather than filled.

## 21. Guilt / can't forgive myself

**Roots: Freedom, Meaninglessness.** Clinical grounding: moral-injury literature (Freedom =
eroded agency/integrity; Meaninglessness = violated conscience as a meaning structure).
**Safety flag carried forward from `kb-21-25.md`: moral-injury-adjacent guilt (self-
condemnation tied to real/perceived serious harm caused, especially in a caregiving or
high-stakes role) is explicitly linked to suicide risk in current literature and should be
treated as an escalation/crisis-redirect candidate, not routed to a philosophy match.**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching

Thich Nhat Hanh (Plum Village, the monastic community he founded) teaches that forgiveness
of others is not possible without first forgiving oneself, and grounds this in mindfulness
practice rather than in erasing the past: "the practice of mindfulness can always transform,
even transform the past," because "the past is still there in the present moment, and you
can touch it." In his commentary on the *Satipatthana Sutta* (the Pali Canon's Sutra on the
Four Establishments of Mindfulness), the instruction to look mindfully and deeply into the
nature of all phenomena — including one's own past actions — is the basis for this practice
of touching and transforming guilt rather than suppressing or being ruled by it. Separately,
Buddhist psychology treats remorse/regret (Pali/Skt *kukkucca*) as a mental factor that can
be either wholesome (spurring genuine amends) or an unwholesome hindrance (repetitive
self-punishment with no corrective value) — the tradition's own distinction between
productive guilt and corrosive guilt.

### B. Shadow work

Robert A. Johnson, in *Owning Your Own Shadow* (a standard post-Jungian shadow-work text),
makes a specifically counter-intuitive argument directly relevant to this category: guilt
itself can be a shadow phenomenon. Johnson argues guilt is "arrogant, because it means you
have already taken a side and declared yourself right" — a premature, one-sided verdict on
oneself that forecloses the harder, more honest work of holding competing truths (I did
wrong / I am also more than that wrong) at once. In Jungian terms, unintegrated guilt can
function as a way of avoiding the shadow rather than facing it — punishing oneself substitutes
for the slower work of actually integrating the disowned part of the self that produced the
harmful action.

### C. Real psychology methodology

Two evidence-based, non-clinical threads, distinct from the moral-injury *treatment*
protocols already covered in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`:
- **Self-forgiveness research** (Robert Enright and the process/REACH forgiveness models,
  synthesized in a 2024 systematic review, "Psychological interventions to promote
  self-forgiveness," *BMC Psychology*) frames self-forgiveness as a process — acknowledgment
  of wrongdoing, empathy/perspective-taking, and eventual self-acceptance — not a single
  decision. Newer mechanism research (2024–2025) identifies a **mindfulness →
  self-compassion → self-forgiveness** mediation pathway, giving a concrete, self-directed
  entry point (mindfulness practice, then self-compassion practice) rather than "just try to
  forgive yourself."
- **Kristin Neff's self-compassion research**: a foundational meta-analysis (MacBeth &
  Gumley, 2012, *Clinical Psychology Review*) found a large effect size (r = -0.54) linking
  higher self-compassion to lower psychopathology (depression, anxiety, stress) across 20
  samples. Neff's model — self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness — and her 5-minute
  "Self-Compassion Break" practice are a distinct, self-directed complement to
  self-forgiveness work specifically because they target self-kindness toward the *person*
  without first requiring resolution of the moral question about the *act*.

### D. Synthesis note

All three pillars converge on the same structural point: real self-forgiveness is a slow,
honest process — acknowledging the wrong, holding compassion for oneself, and doing
repair — never a shortcut that erases accountability. Thich Nhat Hanh's mindfulness practice
supplies the "touch and transform the past" mechanism; Johnson's shadow-work framing warns
against guilt that short-circuits this process by rushing to self-condemnation as false
closure; Enright/Neff's research gives a concrete, sequenced self-directed practice
(mindfulness → self-compassion → self-forgiveness). **Safety note, explicit per
`CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md` and `kb-21-25.md`:** this wisdom content is appropriate for the
non-clinical majority of guilt presentations (everyday regret, "I said something I regret").
It is **not** a substitute for crisis-resource redirection when guilt is moral-injury-adjacent
— tied to a real or perceived serious harm caused in a high-stakes role, paired with
hopelessness ("I don't think I deserve to feel okay again") — which current literature
explicitly ties to suicide risk and which the app's matching layer should route to the
crisis redirect rather than to this material.

## 22. Impatience / waiting for something to change

**Roots: Freedom (well-established), Death (reasonably inferred — "felt pressure of finite
time," explicitly not mortality salience, per `kb-21-25.md`).**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching

The *Tao Te Ching*, chapter 15 (Stephen Mitchell translation), is the clearest primary-text
match for this category, and impatience is arguably the chapter's central image: "Do you
have the patience to wait till your mud settles and the water is clear? Can you remain
unmoving till the right action arises by itself?" This directly names the category's
presenting complaint — the discomfort of waiting for an unresolved situation to
resolve — and offers wu wei (non-forcing action) as the response: not passivity, but ceasing
to agitate the situation so its own resolution can become visible. Mitchell's commentary
connects this to a state "in which the right stroke or the right movement happens by itself,
effortlessly, without any interference of the conscious will" — wu wei as the "purest and
most effective form of action," which reframes impatience not as a flaw to suppress but as
excess agitation that clouds judgment about when real action is actually available.

### B. Shadow work

Marie-Louise von Franz's *The Problem of the Puer Aeternus* (originally a 1959–1960 lecture
series at the C.G. Jung Institute, Zurich) documents the *puer aeternus* ("eternal youth")
archetype, whose defining traits include a pronounced disposition toward impatience: "when
the puer aeternus has to take something seriously, he makes a few poor attempts and then
impatiently gives up," and the type is described as "usually very impatient by disposition,"
with an unwillingness to "bear the weight" of any situation and a persistent urge to keep
all options open rather than commit and wait out a process. From a shadow-work perspective,
chronic impatience can be a signal that the puer pattern (avoidance of the weight/duration of
real commitment) is operating rather than a purely external problem with how slowly the world
is moving.

### C. Real psychology methodology

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy's **values clarification and committed action** processes
(part of Hayes's six-process "Hexaflex" model of psychological flexibility) are a
non-clinical, self-directed complement to the mindfulness-based clinical approaches already
covered in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`. Rather than targeting the waiting/uncertainty directly, ACT
practice asks a person to clarify what they value about the outcome they're waiting for and
to identify committed actions available *now*, independent of the wait's resolution — shifting
the locus of felt control from "when will this end" (outside the person's control, the source
of impatience per Sweeny's process-model research) to "what can I do that matters, regardless
of timeline" (within the person's control). This is a designed extrapolation from ACT's
general mechanism applied to impatience specifically — no ACT trial was found targeting
impatience as a named outcome, so this pillar should be read as principled application of a
validated framework, not a targeted clinical-trial finding.

### D. Synthesis note

The three pillars work at different points in the same arc: the Tao Te Ching names the
felt experience (agitated waiting) and offers a stance (stop stirring the water); von Franz's
puer aeternus diagnosis explains a common underlying pattern when impatience is chronic
rather than situational (avoidance of the weight of real commitment); and ACT's
values/committed-action work gives a concrete self-directed practice for regaining a sense of
agency during a wait that cannot be hastened. No safety-escalation flag was found for this
category in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`; this material is well-suited to the general, low-risk
presentation typical of impatience.

## 23. Low mood / persistent sadness

**Roots: Death, Freedom, Isolation, Meaninglessness — per `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`, this is a
designed synthesis of four separately-sourced literatures, not a single unified claim;
Freedom/Isolation/Meaninglessness are each well-established, Death is real but secondary.**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching

Pema Chödrön's *When Things Fall Apart: Heart Advice for Difficult Times* (a widely cited
work grounded in the Tibetan Buddhist tradition she teaches within, Shambhala) offers a
teaching that maps directly onto persistent sadness without a specific trigger: rather than
treating groundlessness and sorrow as problems to be fixed, the practice is to "move toward
painful situations with friendliness and curiosity, relaxing into the essential
groundlessness of our entire situation." Chödrön frames impermanence — that "things are
always in transition" — as the reason lasting escape from sadness through control or
avoidance isn't available, and reframes the difficulty itself as workable: "the healing comes
from letting there be room for all of this to happen: room for grief, for relief, for misery,
for joy." This speaks to the Death (impermanence), Freedom (relinquishing the demand for
control over the situation), and Isolation (the instruction to stay present with one's own
experience rather than flee it) roots at once.

### B. Shadow work

James Hollis, a Jungian analyst trained at the C.G. Jung Institute Zurich (1977–1982) and
author of over twenty books on depth psychology, addresses low mood directly in
*Swamplands of the Soul: New Life in Dismal Places*. Hollis's central thesis reframes
depression not as pure pathology but as material the psyche is presenting for a reason: "it
is precisely where we encounter the 'gravitas' of life that we also uncover its purpose, its
dignity and its deepest meaning," and "doubt and depression... provide us with disempowering
messages, but under each is a summons to an action, especially when we don't feel like it."
The book's premise — that the goal of a life is not happiness but meaning, and that the
"swamplands" (grief, anxiety, depression, loneliness) are where genuine psychological growth
actually happens — gives low mood a Meaninglessness-root-relevant reframe distinct from
purely symptom-reduction framing.

### C. Real psychology methodology

**Gratitude interventions** (Emmons & McCullough, 2003, *Journal of Personality and Social
Psychology* — the foundational "counting blessings vs. burdens" study) are a well-replicated,
non-clinical, self-directed practice distinct from the behavioral-activation/CBT protocols
already covered in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`. In the original study, participants who kept
weekly gratitude journals (listing 3–5 things they were grateful for) reported feeling
better about their lives and more optimistic than comparison groups; a 2023 systematic review
and meta-analysis (PubMed 37585888) confirms gratitude interventions produce better mental
health outcomes and improved mood across a substantial evidence base, with the effect on
positive affect being the most robust finding.

### D. Synthesis note

The three pillars address low mood from complementary angles: Chödrön's teaching offers a
stance toward the sadness itself (befriend it, don't flee it — directly addressing Death and
Freedom); Hollis's shadow-work framing supplies a Meaninglessness-oriented reason not to treat
the mood purely as malfunction; and gratitude-journaling research offers a small, concrete,
evidence-based daily practice a person can start immediately. No category-specific
escalation flag was found for #23 in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md` (unlike #24 below); nonetheless,
this content should be positioned as a complement to, not a replacement for, clinical
treatment when low mood is severe, prolonged, or functionally impairing — the taxonomy's
existing crisis-redirect carve-outs (active suicidal ideation, self-harm) apply regardless of
category.

- [When Things Fall Apart: Tibetan Buddhist Nun and Teacher Pema Chödrön on Transformation Through Difficult Times — The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/07/17/when-things-fall-apart-pema-chodron/)
- [Swamplands of the Soul: New Life in Dismal Places — James Hollis, publisher/overview](https://www.psychceu.com/hollis/swamplands.asp)

## 24. Can't feel joy / anhedonia

**Roots: Death, Freedom, Isolation (now anhedonia-specific-evidenced per `kb-21-25.md`),
Meaninglessness.** **Safety flag carried forward: state/recent-onset anhedonia is an
independent predictor of suicidal ideation, separate from overall depression severity
(Ducasse et al. meta-analysis) — per `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`, this category should not be
treated as "lower stakes" than #23; sudden/recent loss of pleasure or interest deserves at
least equal, and per some literature greater, crisis-redirect sensitivity than sadness-coded
language.**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching

Thich Nhat Hanh's *Present Moment, Wonderful Moment: Mindfulness Verses for Daily Living*
(Parallax Press) is built around gathas (short mindfulness verses) designed to reconnect
attention with ordinary, already-available sources of joy rather than to chase new or bigger
pleasures — directly relevant to anhedonia's presentation as a *disconnection from available*
pleasure rather than an absence of pleasurable things. A central verse: "Breathing in, I calm
my body. Breathing out, I smile. Dwelling in the present moment, I know this is a wonderful
moment," paired with Thich Nhat Hanh's framing that "you have to make the present moment into
the most wonderful moment of your life" — the practice is attention and presence, not
acquisition. This distinguishes the teaching from a "go find something to be happy about"
framing, instead targeting the attentional/presence mechanism that anhedonia specifically
disrupts.

### B. Shadow work

Jung's own concept of libido as general psychic energy (not narrowly sexual, but the "life
force, or passion that drives us forward" across creative, intellectual, and instinctive
domains) gives a Jungian account of anhedonia specifically, distinct from Hollis's
meaning-focused framing of low mood (#23 above): in Jungian theory, depression/anhedonia
occurs when "the unconscious has withdrawn libido from the conscious world," so that "in a
depression, our vitality sinks downward into the unconscious, as if it has run away from us" —
but "libido is never lost... it just gets trapped somewhere in the unconscious." This is a
meaningfully different shadow-work frame than low mood's "summons to meaning": anhedonia here
is framed as energy that has been withdrawn and redirected inward (often toward an unattended
complex or unlived part of the personality) rather than lost, with the therapeutic
implication (per this same literature) that recovering interest/pleasure requires locating
and integrating whatever the withdrawn energy is currently bound up in, not simply willing
oneself to feel more.

### C. Real psychology methodology

**Savoring research** (Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff, *Savoring: A New Model of Positive
Experience*, 2007) is a non-clinical, self-directed practice distinct from the clinical
Positive Affect Treatment protocols already covered in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`. Savoring is
defined as intentionally attending to, appreciating, and prolonging positive experience as it
happens (rather than letting it pass unnoticed); the research base specifically links
savoring capacity to **lower levels of anhedonia and depression**, and positive psychologists
have adapted savoring techniques as self-directed tools for people experiencing emotional
deficits associated with depression and hopelessness. This is a distinct mechanism from
gratitude journaling (#23's pillar C, which is retrospective/reflective) — savoring is
present-moment and experience-focused, which fits anhedonia's specific deficit (reduced
capacity to register pleasure as it occurs) better than a backward-looking gratitude practice.

### D. Synthesis note

Thich Nhat Hanh's presence-based gathas, Jungian libido-withdrawal theory, and savoring
research converge on a shared insight: anhedonia is not best approached as "find bigger
things to be happy about" but as a disruption in the *attentional/energetic connection* to
pleasure that is already present — which is why all three pillars emphasize noticing and
staying with small available positive experience rather than pursuit or acquisition. **Safety
note, explicit and load-bearing for this category per `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`:** this wisdom
content is appropriate for the non-clinical majority of anhedonia presentations (chronic,
stable, low-grade loss of interest). It is emphatically **not** a substitute for
crisis-resource redirection when the anhedonia is sudden or recently worsened ("I don't feel
excited about anything anymore, not even things I used to look forward to, and it just
started a couple weeks ago") — current literature ties that specific pattern to suicide-risk
signal independent of overall depression severity, and the app's matching layer should treat
that language as at least as high a priority for crisis-redirect as any sadness-coded
language, per the explicit recommendation in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md` and `kb-21-25.md`.

## 25. Being mistreated / bullied / disrespected

**Roots: Isolation, Freedom — well-established, now with boss-perpetrator/power-imbalance
evidence per `kb-21-25.md`. Severity-tiering note carried forward: chronic/severe bullying
may present as complex PTSD; this category was not found to carry an independent
suicide-risk-marker literature the way anhedonia does — the risk pathway runs through
depression/PTSD as intermediate steps — so it is a severity-tiering matter, not a hard
crisis-level flag, but professional support alongside philosophy should be signaled for
chronic/severe presentations.**

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching

The Buddhist teaching of the **Eight Worldly Winds** (*loka-dhamma*, grounded in the Pali
Canon, discussed in relation to the *Brahmajala Sutta*) names praise and blame as one of four
paired, unavoidable conditions of life (alongside gain/loss, pleasure/pain, fame/disrepute)
that "ceaselessly change" and that compound suffering specifically when a person becomes
attached to either pole. The practical instruction, per this teaching, is not to suppress
one's reaction to mistreatment but to test it: "Is this true? Is it based on fact? Will it
lead to liberation?" — a discernment practice that lets a person separate what's worth
taking in from what's simply another person's projection or judgment. The Dalai Lama's
teaching on this same territory offers a specific practice for the moment of being
disrespected: "when somebody says something unpleasant or hurtful to us, we need to learn to
be patient and forbearing and remind ourselves that their words are just like the sounds of
an echo, equally insubstantial and unreal" — directly targeting the Freedom root (reclaiming
agency over one's own reaction rather than being controlled by another's words).

### B. Shadow work

Sylvia Brinton Perera's *The Scapegoat Complex: Toward a Mythology of Shadow and Guilt* (a
Jungian analyst's book-length treatment, published in the Studies in Jungian Psychology by
Jungian Analysts series) gives a specific, sourced account of chronic mistreatment as a
shadow phenomenon on the *perpetrator's* side: scapegoating is "a way of denying one's own
dark side by projecting it onto others," carried out by people who are "themselves often
wounded" and who "have driven their own shadows underground from where such are projected
onto the victim." This reframe is directly useful for category 25 without blaming the
target: understanding that mistreatment often functions as the mistreater's unprocessed
shadow material being displaced onto a convenient target can reduce a victim's tendency
toward self-blame ("what's wrong with me that this keeps happening") — Perera's work
documents that the scapegoated person, without this framing, often internalizes "felt
unworthiness" and "transpersonal guilt" that was never theirs to carry.

### C. Real psychology methodology

**Kristin Neff's self-compassion research** (see also pillar C of category 21, applied here
to a different mechanism) is directly relevant as a distinct, non-clinical, self-directed
practice for the individual-level work of category 25: MacBeth & Gumley's 2012 meta-analysis
(r = -0.54 linking self-compassion to lower psychopathology) supports self-compassion practice
as a buffer against the self-esteem erosion that chronic mistreatment produces. This is
distinct from the clinical-practice interventions already covered in `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`
(cognitive rehearsal, assertiveness/boundary-setting training, organizational-level
intervention) — self-compassion practice targets how a person relates to themselves *after*
an incident of mistreatment, rather than how to prevent or respond to the incident itself,
and Neff's own framing (self-compassion is partly rooted in a Theravada Buddhist
understanding of common humanity) gives it a natural bridge to pillar A's equanimity
teaching.

### D. Synthesis note

The three pillars target three different moments in a mistreatment experience: the Eight
Worldly Winds teaching addresses the moment of the disrespect itself (don't hand your sense
of worth over to another's words); Perera's scapegoat-complex shadow work addresses the
after-the-fact meaning-making (this is often about the other person's unprocessed shadow, not
a verdict on you); and self-compassion research gives an ongoing, evidence-based practice for
rebuilding self-regard eroded by repeated mistreatment. Consistent with `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`,
this material fits the majority, single-incident-to-moderate end of this category well; for
chronic, power-imbalanced bullying with signs of functional collapse or possible complex-PTSD
features, the app's matching layer should pair this content with a signal that professional
support may help alongside — not instead of — the philosophical material, consistent with the
severity-tiering recommendation already on record for this category.

  well-sourced for impatience-as-a-pattern, but no source was found linking it to Yalom's
  *Death* root specifically (consistent with `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`'s own finding that the
  Death-root mapping for this category is a designed inference, not a sourced claim). Flagged
  here rather than forcing a Death-root connection into the shadow-work pillar that the
  sourcing doesn't support.
- **#22 Impatience, pillar C (methodology):** the ACT values-clarification/committed-action
  application to impatience specifically is a principled extrapolation from ACT's general,
  well-validated mechanism, not a targeted clinical trial or study naming impatience as an
  outcome. Stated as such in pillar C above rather than overstated as directly evidenced.
- **#24 Anhedonia, pillar B (shadow work):** Jung's libido-withdrawal account of depression
  broadly is well documented, but the search did not surface a source applying it to
  anhedonia *by that specific clinical name* (as opposed to depression generally) — the
  application to anhedonia specifically in pillar B above is a reasonable, clearly-labeled
  extrapolation from Jung's general theory of libido/depression, not a source that names
  anhedonia directly.
- No category in this batch was left without real sourcing for all three pillars; the items
  above are noted for precision, not because a pillar was left empty or invented.
