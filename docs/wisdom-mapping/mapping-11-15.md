# Wisdom mapping — v1.0 categories 11–15

**Scope.** This file maps real, sourced content across three pillars — Eastern philosophy/metaphysical teaching, shadow work, and evidence-based non-clinical psychology methodology — for exactly the five categories below, per [`TAXONOMY_V1.0.md`](../TAXONOMY_V1.0.md) and [`CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`](../CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md). Nothing here duplicates the clinical/diagnostic treatment already documented in the clinical KB or in `docs/research/kb-11-15.md`; this file is the wisdom-guidance layer that sits alongside it. Every claim is either a verified real citation of a passage/verse, or an accurately described teaching/framework with a citation to a real source describing it — no invented quotes, verses, or techniques. Other v1.0 categories are out of scope and were not touched.

## 11. Compulsive / behavioral coping
**Roots:** Death, Meaninglessness

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The Buddhist Second Noble Truth (*samudaya*) locates the origin of suffering in **taṇhā** — craving, an insatiable "thirst" for sense pleasure, for becoming, or for annihilation/escape — which is precisely the mechanism compulsive behaviors (gambling, shopping, bingeing, doomscrolling) run on: relief-seeking that intensifies the very craving it tries to satisfy ([Access to Insight, "Taṇhā — Craving"](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/ptf/dhamma/sacca/sacca2/tanha.html)). Thich Nhat Hanh's *The Heart of the Buddha's Teaching* extends this into a practical teaching on **habit energy** (Vietnamese *tập khí*): compulsive patterns are described not as moral failures to be willed away but as energies that formed through repetition and must be met with mindful recognition ("hello, my habit energy") rather than either indulgence or suppression — the Buddhist middle path applied directly to compulsion.

### B. Shadow work
Carl Jung's own engagement with addiction is documented in his January 1961 letter to Bill Wilson, co-founder of Alcoholics Anonymous (reproduced by several AA-affiliated archives). Jung told Wilson that a patient's "craving for alcohol was the equivalent, on a low level, of the spiritual thirst of our being for wholeness... the union with God," coining the phrase **spiritus contra spiritum** ("spirit against spirits") — the idea that compulsive craving is a distorted, substitute search for the wholeness that shadow-integration (not indulgence) actually provides ([Spiritus Contra Spiritum — Jung's letter to Bill Wilson, AA Central Office of Salt Lake](https://www.saltlakeaa.org/2015/01/spiritus-contra-spiritum-carl-jungs-letter-to-bill-wilson-january-30-1961/)). This connects to Jung's formal definition of the shadow in *Aion* (1951, Collected Works vol. 9ii, ¶14) as "a moral problem that challenges the whole ego-personality, for no one can become conscious of the shadow without considerable moral effort" — compulsive coping can be read, in this literature, as a way of avoiding that effort rather than doing it.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Kristin Neff's self-compassion research (three components: self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness) is directly relevant here because shame is a documented relapse driver: shame's shift from "I did something wrong" to "I am something wrong" feeds hopelessness and is identified in addiction-recovery literature as one of the biggest relapse triggers, while self-compassion is empirically linked to lower shame ([Self-Compassion: Theory, Method, Research, and Intervention — Neff, *Annual Review of Psychology*, 2023](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Neff-2023.pdf); [Self-Compassion and Addiction Recovery](https://www.sanctuarytreatmentcenter.com/self-compassion/)). This is a self-directed, non-clinical framework (Mindful Self-Compassion) distinct from the CBT/relapse-prevention treatment already documented as the clinical standard of care.

### D. Synthesis
The Buddhist teaching names the mechanism (craving as a self-perpetuating thirst), the Jungian material reframes that thirst as a distorted search for wholeness that shadow work — not more craving-satisfaction — actually answers, and self-compassion practice offers a concrete, non-shaming daily practice for interrupting the shame-relapse cycle. **Safety note carried forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`:** this category has a hard ceiling — severe presentations carry real comorbidity (82.2%) and elevated suicide risk (gambling disorder specifically), so philosophy/shadow-work content should be offered as an affect-regulation/meaning companion for non-crisis presentations, with explicit crisis-resource redirection built in, not a substitute for care in severe cases.

## 12. Betrayal / broken trust
**Root:** Isolation

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
Pema Chödrön's teaching on **groundlessness**, developed in *When Things Fall Apart* and explicitly rooted in her own experience of her husband's infidelity, holds that the impulse to flee groundlessness (grasping for a new certainty, a story, or revenge) perpetuates suffering, while "leaning in" — moving toward the raw, ungrounded feeling with friendliness and curiosity rather than running from it — is the path through betrayal's disorientation ([The Marginalian, "When Things Fall Apart"](https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/07/17/when-things-fall-apart-pema-chodron/); [Pema Chödrön Foundation](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/when-things-fall-apart-book/)). This is a real, well-documented teaching from a named contemporary Tibetan Buddhist (Shambhala tradition) teacher, not a fabricated quote.

### B. Shadow work
Robert A. Johnson's *Owning Your Own Shadow* describes the **golden shadow**: in falling in love or extending trust, we project our own disowned brilliance and wholeness onto another person, who then carries an impossible "10,000-volt" charge; when that person acts in a merely human, fallible, or betraying way, the projection collapses, and the pain is partly the shock of that collapse, not only the act itself. Connie Zweig and Steve Wolf's *Romancing the Shadow* extends this directly to betrayal: unintegrated shadow material (disowned rage, shame, need) is what "causes us to betray our loved ones as well as ourselves," and betrayal from another activates the wounded, distrustful parts of our own shadow that projection had been keeping at bay ([Romancing the Shadow — publisher/review summary](https://www.spiritualityandpractice.com/book-reviews/view/1393/romancing-the-shadow); [Owning Your Own Shadow — excerpt/summary](https://www.spiritualityandpractice.com/book-reviews/view/7765/owning-your-own-shadow)).

### C. Real psychology methodology
Post-traumatic growth (PTG) research, originated by Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, has been specifically applied to betrayal and infidelity: PTG following relational betrayal shows up across the model's five domains — appreciation of life, relationships with others, new possibilities, personal strength, and spiritual change — with growth often mediated by meaning-making, not by minimizing what happened ([Post-Traumatic Growth and Post-Traumatic Stress after Infidelity, Encyclopedia MDPI](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/50412); [Posttraumatic Growth in Relationally Betrayed Women — Laaser](https://cdn.ymaws.com/iitap.com/resource/resmgr/arie_files/m2-ptsd_growth_women-laaser-.pdf)). This is a positive-psychology research framework, distinct from clinical betrayal-trauma treatment.

### D. Synthesis
Chödrön's groundlessness names what betrayal actually feels like from the inside (the ground giving way) and offers a stance toward it that doesn't require immediate resolution; the shadow-work literature explains *why* betrayal cuts so deep (a collapsed golden-shadow projection, and one's own disowned material getting activated); PTG research shows that real, researched growth is possible on the other side without requiring premature forgiveness. **Safety/accuracy note carried forward:** `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md` flags that the secondary Meaninglessness-root hook for this category is still an unconfirmed inference — content here stays anchored to the well-established Isolation root and should not overclaim a meaninglessness angle as settled.

## 13. General life meaninglessness
**Root:** Meaninglessness

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The Bhagavad Gita's central karma-yoga teaching, Chapter 2, verse 47 (*karmaṇy-evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana*) — "you have a right to your actions, but never to the fruits of those actions" — addresses existential meaninglessness at its root: it relocates meaning away from outcomes (which are never fully in one's control and can always feel arbitrary or pointless) and into the quality of engaged action itself ([Bhagavad-gītā As It Is 2.47, Bhaktivedanta Book Trust/vedabase.io](https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/47/); [BG 2.47, Swami Mukundananda translation](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/2/verse/47/)). This is a directly citable verse from a real text, not a paraphrase presented as scripture.

### B. Shadow work
Jungian analyst James Hollis's *Finding Meaning in the Second Half of Life* argues that the felt meaninglessness many people encounter at midlife is not a malfunction but a structural transition: the first half of life is organized around acquisition and adaptation to others' expectations, and its scripts stop producing meaning once outgrown; individuation — consciously engaging one's own shadow material rather than continuing to perform an outgrown persona — is what generates a second, self-authored sense of meaning ([Finding Meaning in the Second Half of Life — review/summary](https://www.spiritualityandpractice.com/book-reviews/view/21109/finding-meaning-in-the-second-half-of-life)). Jung's own clinical observation, from *Modern Man in Search of a Soul* (1933), states it directly: "Among all my patients in the second half of life... there has not been one whose problem in the last resort was not that of finding a religious [meaning-making] outlook on life... every one of them fell ill because he had lost what the living religions of every age have given their followers" (Ch. 11, "Psychotherapists or the Clergy," p. 229) — a real, verifiable, widely-cited Jung passage.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Martin Seligman's PERMA model (*Flourish*, 2011) treats Meaning — "belonging to and serving something larger than the self" — as one of five independently-measured, empirically-supported pillars of flourishing (alongside Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, and Accomplishment), giving a non-clinical, self-assessable framework distinct from Steger's clinical Presence-of-Meaning construct already used in the taxonomy's clinical grounding ([Positive Psychology Center, PERMA Theory of Well-Being, University of Pennsylvania](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/learn-more/perma-theory-well-being-and-perma-workshops); [PERMA psychometric properties study, PMC 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11290191/)).

### D. Synthesis
The Gita relocates meaning from outcome to action, Hollis/Jung explain why an old source of meaning stops working and frame the felt emptiness as an invitation to individuate rather than a symptom to eliminate, and PERMA gives a practical, multi-pillar way to actively build meaning back into daily life. `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md` notes this category's distinctness from depression is now better evidenced — content here should stay in that lane (existential/meaning-focused) and not substitute for depression screening when low mood is also present.

## 14. Career / vocational purpose
**Root:** Meaninglessness

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The Bhagavad Gita's teaching on **svadharma** (one's own duty/nature-aligned path), Chapter 3, verse 35 — "it is far better to perform one's own duties imperfectly than to master the duties of another" (*śreyān sva-dharmo viguṇaḥ para-dharmāt sv-anuṣṭhitāt*) — speaks directly to career/vocational confusion: it frames vocational fit as alignment with one's own nature rather than a competitive comparison against others' paths, and treats even an imperfectly-executed authentic vocation as more sound than a well-executed borrowed one ([Bhagavad-gītā As It Is 3.35, vedabase.io](https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/35/); [BG 3.35, Swami Mukundananda translation](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/3/verse/35/)).

### B. Shadow work
Hollis's *Finding Meaning in the Second Half of Life* draws an explicit distinction between **career** (socially defined, adaptive, first-half-of-life) and **vocation** (the soul's own calling, which requires confronting the shadow material — fear, others' expectations, an outgrown persona — that a purely career-driven life was built to avoid): "the soul carries a purpose distinct from career; aligning work with that calling transforms it into vocation" ([Finding Meaning in the Second Half of Life — summary](https://www.spiritualityandpractice.com/book-reviews/view/21109/finding-meaning-in-the-second-half-of-life)). This is the same author as category 13's shadow-work source but a distinct, career-specific argument from the same book, cited separately here.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Mark Savickas's **Career Construction Theory** and its applied practice, **Life Design counseling**, is the current dominant paradigm in vocational psychology (2024 *Frontiers in Psychology* review): it treats vocational identity as three layers — vocational personality ("what"), career adaptability ("how"), and life theme ("why") — and uses self-directed narrative/autobiographical exercises (not clinical treatment) to help a person construct, rather than simply discover, a sense of vocational purpose ([Career construction theory: tools, interventions, and future trends — Frontiers in Psychology/PMC, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11026660/); [Life Design Counseling — Savickas, Journal of Counseling & Development](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/Life_Design_Counseling.pdf)).

### D. Synthesis
The Gita's svadharma teaching gives a philosophical reason to stop comparing one's path to others'; Hollis's career/vocation distinction explains what's missing when a career "works" on paper but feels hollow; Savickas's Life Design gives a concrete, self-directed method (narrative/life-theme exercises) for actually constructing that missing vocational layer. Per `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`, this category is well-established as distinct from #13 and sits in non-clinical, career-counseling territory — safe ground for philosophy-and-life-design content with no diagnostic threshold implicated, though it can co-occur with clinical presentations (e.g., #10/#11) without itself being one.

## 15. Overwhelm / too much to control
**Root:** Freedom

### A. Eastern philosophy / metaphysical teaching
The Tao Te Ching's teaching of **wu wei** (non-forcing/non-striving action), stated in Chapter 43 — "the softest thing in the world overcomes the hardest thing in the world... thus I know the value of non-action" — is the central Taoist response to the felt need to control or force outcomes: water, the text's recurring image, prevails not through effort but through yielding and working with what already is. Sinologist Edward Slingerland's *Trying Not to Try* (Crown, 2015) is a real, scholarly (University of British Columbia, Canada Research Chair in Chinese Thought) treatment of exactly this teaching, connecting classical wu wei to contemporary psychology of effortless action and spontaneity ([Tao Te Ching Ch. 43, multiple translations](https://www.egreenway.com/taoism/ttclz43.htm); [Trying Not to Try — Edward Slingerland](https://www.edwardslingerland.com/trying-not-to-try)).

### B. Shadow work
Marion Woodman — a Jungian analyst trained at the C.G. Jung Institute Zürich — argues in *Addiction to Perfection: The Still Unravished Bride* (1982) that compulsive perfectionism and the drive to control everything is itself "the master addiction from which all others derive": an over-functioning persona built to manage an underlying, disowned sense of not being enough, explored through case studies and dreams from her analytic practice ([Addiction to Perfection — overview, Seba](https://www.seba.health/library/recovery/woodman-addiction-to-perfection/); [Marion Woodman, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marion_Woodman)). Read through this lens, chronic overwhelm/over-control can be shadow material (a compensatory over-functioning) rather than only a practical bandwidth problem.

### C. Real psychology methodology
Steven Hayes's Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) framework — specifically its non-clinical, self-directed **values clarification and committed action** processes within the psychological-flexibility ("Hexaflex") model — offers a distinct, evidence-based practice: accepting what genuinely cannot be controlled while identifying values and taking small, values-aligned committed actions within what remains controllable ([How Does ACT Work? — positivepsychology.com](https://positivepsychology.com/act-acceptance-and-commitment-therapy/); [ACT and Psychological Well-Being: A Narrative Review, PMC 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11837766/)). This is deliberately distinct from the workplace-burnout-specific CBT treatment already documented as the clinical standard for this category.

### D. Synthesis
Wu wei reframes control itself as often counterproductive ("the softest overcomes the hardest"); Woodman's shadow-work lens asks whether the compulsion to control everything is compensating for a deeper disowned fear; ACT's values/committed-action work gives a concrete practice for acting effectively on what's controllable while genuinely releasing what isn't. **Safety/scope note carried forward from `CLINICAL_KB_V1.0.md`:** ICD-11 classifies burnout strictly as an occupational phenomenon and explicitly says the term "should not be applied to describe experiences in other areas of life," and the evidence base for this category is workplace/burnout-specific — the non-workplace, "too much to control" framing used here (and the wu wei/ACT content) is reasonably extrapolated, not directly clinically validated for non-occupational presentations, and should be flagged as such rather than presented as equally well-evidenced.
