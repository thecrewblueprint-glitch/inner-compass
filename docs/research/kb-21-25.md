## 21. Guilt / can't forgive myself

**v1.0 roots: Freedom, Meaninglessness** (v0.1 #17)

### Currency check (2024–2026)

Nothing overturns the first pass, but there is real 2024–2025 movement that sharpens it:

- **Self-forgiveness intervention evidence has matured from "under-researched" to
  "reviewed."** A 2024 systematic review in *BMC Psychology* — "Psychological
  interventions to promote self-forgiveness" — screened 399 articles down to 19 eligible
  intervention studies, i.e. there is now enough of a literature to systematically
  review, which wasn't really true when the first pass called self-forgiveness "the
  stepchild of forgiveness research."
- A 2024–2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of forgiveness training programs in
  university contexts (*Cogent*, 2024) and a broader 2025 *Frontiers in Psychology*
  paper on forgiveness as a solution to interpersonal conflict add further recent
  evidence that structured forgiveness/self-forgiveness training produces measurable
  gains in empathy, self-esteem, and hope, with reductions in anxiety and depression.
- New mechanism research: a 2024–2025 study found **mindfulness → self-compassion →
  self-forgiveness** is a mediation pathway, giving a concrete, evidence-based technique
  chain (mindfulness training, then self-compassion practice) rather than "just try to
  forgive yourself."
- Moral injury: the field is now explicitly extending past military populations. 2025–2026
  work (*Journal of Religion and Health*, "Moral Injury as a Wound of Meaning and
  Conscience," 2026; a 2026 *Personality and Social Psychology*-adjacent healthcare-worker
  intervention paper; a 2025 *Translational Psychiatry*-style *Frontiers in Psychiatry*
  qualitative study of expert perspectives) confirms moral injury as a live, expanding
  research area in healthcare, not a closed topic — and newly emphasizes **"relational
  reorientation"** (a November 2025 PubMed paper flags "moral injury research at a
  crossroads," arguing current models over-individualize what is often a relational/
  systemic injury). This nuance matters for how a guidance app should frame the problem:
  not purely "fix your internal guilt" but "your sense of violated values may be
  relational/contextual, not solely a personal failing."
- Moral injury is now explicitly tied to suicide risk in current literature ("Moral
  Injury: The Hidden Wound Driving Suicide and Despair," *Psychology Today*, 2025), which
  is directly relevant to this app's crisis-redirect threshold (see Real-world practice,
  below).

**Net effect on the taxonomy:** no structural change needed. The Freedom + Meaninglessness
mapping is, if anything, more strongly supported than in the first pass.

### Gap-fill

The first pass already flagged one boundary issue (guilt vs. shame routing) and resolved
it well; no new inference gaps were introduced. Two smaller items worth documenting
explicitly for the matching layer:

1. **Self-forgiveness is not unconditionally good** — this was noted in the first pass
   (Woodyatt & Wenzel) and 2025 sources reaffirm it: self-forgiveness reached prematurely,
   without genuine acknowledgment of wrongdoing, can undermine accountability. A philosophy
   guidance app should avoid surfacing "let yourself off the hook" material without a
   companion emphasis on genuine acknowledgment/repair — this is consistent with, not a
   contradiction of, most Eastern ethical frameworks (which typically pair
   compassion-toward-self with honest reckoning, not bypassing it).
2. **Moral injury is the strongest empirical bridge for the Freedom+Meaninglessness
   mapping**, and current (2025–2026) sources make that bridge even more explicit than the
   first pass found — moral injury researchers now use language of "eroded integrity,"
   "wound of meaning and conscience," and "loss of trust in a moral compass," which map
   almost one-to-one onto Yalom's Freedom (responsibility/agency for one's acts) and
   Meaninglessness (conscience/integrity as a meaning structure). This is well-established,
   not merely inferred.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Self-forgiveness–specific interventions**: structured multi-session self-forgiveness
  protocols (Enright-model process-based approaches; the 2024 BMC Psychology review's 19
  studies), typically combining acknowledgment of wrongdoing, empathy/perspective-taking,
  self-compassion practice, and behavioral repair/amends steps. Mindfulness-based
  self-compassion training is a current evidence-based entry point.
  Efficacy: forgiveness-focused psychotherapy shows a pooled positive effect on
  forgiveness itself and secondary reductions in depression/anxiety symptoms, with effect
  sizes broadly in the small-to-moderate range across meta-analyses (both the older
  Wade et al. 2014 psychotherapy meta-analysis and 2024–2025 reviews).
- **Moral-injury-specific approaches** (relevant where guilt is trauma-adjacent, not just
  everyday regret): Adaptive Disclosure, Impact of Killing, Restore and Rebuild, and
  spiritually-integrated Cognitive Processing Therapy — all emphasize values clarification,
  self-compassion, and moral repair rather than pure fear-extinction (distinguishing
  moral-injury treatment from standard PTSD-fear-based protocols).
- **Prevalence/epidemiology**: guilt/self-forgiveness difficulty does not have its own
  formal epidemiological base rate (it is not a DSM diagnosis), but moral injury —
  the clinically severe end of this category — has been specifically linked to suicidal
  ideation and attempts in "high-stress, service-oriented occupations" (military,
  healthcare, first responders), per 2025–2026 sources. This is the clearest signal for
  where this category's presenting concerns can escalate past what a philosophy-guidance
  app should handle: **persistent, severe guilt language with self-condemnation, especially
  from a moral-injury-adjacent context (having caused/failed to prevent serious harm),
  should be treated as a candidate for the app's existing crisis-redirect threshold**, not
  routed straight to philosophical source material.
- Guilt is common as a normal-range emotion (nearly universal), so most presenting
  complaints in this category will be sub-clinical everyday guilt/regret, not moral
  injury — the app should reserve escalation for the more severe end of the spectrum
  described above.

### Case grounding

No single published case report matches "ordinary guilt / can't forgive myself" cleanly
(most published case material in this space is moral-injury-specific, e.g. cognitive
therapy case studies for moral-injury-related PTSD in healthcare workers). Two
illustrative composites, clearly labeled:

- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case) — everyday self-forgiveness failure.**
  A user types: "I said something cruel to my mom before she died and I can't get past it
  even though it's been two years." This matches the pattern described across the
  self-forgiveness literature (Enright; Hall & Fincham): an acknowledged wrong, ongoing
  self-resentment, absence of the "process from self-alienation to being at ease with
  oneself" that defines completed self-forgiveness.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case) — moral-injury-adjacent, escalation
  candidate.** A user types: "I was a nurse during a bad shift and I think my mistake is
  why a patient died. I don't think I deserve to feel okay again." This pattern (acute
  guilt tied to a real/perceived harm caused in a high-stakes caregiving role, self-
  condemnation, hopelessness about ever feeling okay) matches the moral-injury profile
  in current healthcare-worker literature and — per the suicide-risk association noted
  above — is a case where the app's crisis-redirect logic should be triggered rather than
  routed to a philosophy match.

## 22. Impatience / waiting for something to change

**v1.0 roots: Freedom, Death** (v0.1 #18)

### Currency check (2024–2026)

This category's underlying research program is small but genuinely active in exactly the
2024–2026 window, and the first pass's own key citation (Roberts & Fishbach) is joined by
new work from the same lead researcher (Kate Sweeny, UC Riverside):

- **Sweeny (2025), "On (Im)Patience: A New Approach to an Old Virtue"** (*Personality and
  Social Psychology Review*) formalizes a **process model of patience**: impatience is
  reframed as a discrete, common, unpleasant emotion, and patience as a *targeted form of
  emotion regulation* applied to that emotion — not simply "being calm." This is a
  meaningfully more precise clinical/theoretical frame than existed at the first pass.
- **Sweeny, Hawes & Karaman, "When Time Is the Enemy: An Initial Test of the Process
  Model of Patience"** (2026) empirically tests the model (N = 1,401 across three studies,
  2022–2023 data), confirming impatience arises from *perceived unfairness/
  unreasonableness of a delay*, with situational and trait predictors.
- **Hawes & Sweeny (2026)** specifically test **mindfulness** as a mechanism in daily
  patience/impatience — new evidence for a concrete intervention lever (mindfulness
  practice reduces state impatience day-to-day), which is directly useful for a philosophy-
  matching app since mindfulness-based material is a natural fit for this category.
- **Hardisty (2026), "Impatience for negative experiences"** (*Journal of Consumer
  Psychology*) and Sweeny's parallel 2026 consumer-psychology paper extend the model to a
  new domain (dread of negative outcomes, not just eagerness for positive ones) — relevant
  because "waiting for something to change" in this app's category plausibly includes both
  waiting for good news and dreading bad news.
- No DSM/ICD change is applicable — impatience remains a non-diagnostic construct, as in
  the first pass. Explicitly: **nothing in the diagnostic/nosological sense changed**;
  the change is in the dedicated psychological-science literature getting notably more
  developed and precise.

**Net effect on the taxonomy:** no structural change. If anything, the "impatience is a
distinct, non-anxiety construct with its own mechanism" conclusion from the first pass is
now on firmer footing, since there's a named, tested theoretical model (the process model
of patience) rather than a handful of scattered findings.

### Gap-fill

The first pass flagged the Death-root mapping as "a reasonable secondary inference...
rather than a source explicitly naming Death/mortality." Targeted search did not find any
literature — 2024–2026 or earlier — that explicitly frames impatience in terms of
mortality/death anxiety. The new process-model literature reinforces the mapping's
*mechanism* (impatience is fundamentally about the subjective press of time toward an
end-point — the "nearness effect," discomfort intensifying as a wait nears its end) but
still does not use Death/mortality language. **Conclusion: this remains a designed
inference, not a directly sourced claim.** Recommendation: keep Freedom as the strong,
well-evidenced primary root (blame/control/unfairness is squarely Yalom's Freedom
territory) and keep Death as a secondary root, but document in the taxonomy notes that
Death here specifically means "the felt pressure of finite/passing time toward an
outcome," not mortality salience — consistent with the existing matching-layer note for
category #9 (Fear of failure) which makes the same distinction for its own Death link.

### Real-world clinical practice

- Impatience is not itself a treatment target in formal clinical practice (no dedicated
  ICD/DSM code, no dedicated clinical guideline), but the adjacent, well-established
  clinical target is **intolerance of uncertainty (IU)**, treated within CBT for
  generalized anxiety disorder and related conditions. A 2023–2024 meta-analysis
  ("Targeting intolerance of uncertainty in treatment," *ScienceDirect*) found CBT
  protocols centered on IU show good efficacy (cited real-world protocols report ~70%
  full remission from GAD following IU-focused CBT). Techniques directly transferable to
  "impatience" presentations: behavioral experiments testing catastrophic beliefs about
  waiting, and structured "waiting experiments" (deliberately delaying reassurance-seeking
  to build tolerance).
  Caution for the app: this is the evidence base for a *related but distinct* construct
  (uncertainty, not impatience per se) — the first pass already correctly flagged that
  impatience persists even when uncertainty is resolved (e.g., after a vote is cast, before
  the result is announced), so IU-focused CBT technique transfer is reasonable but not a
  perfect fit.
- Mindfulness-based emotion regulation (now directly evidenced for impatience specifically,
  per Hawes & Sweeny 2026) is the most directly on-target evidence-based approach.
- No formal prevalence/epidemiology data exists for "impatience" as a standalone
  presenting concern (unlike depression or bullying, it is not tracked as a population
  health statistic) — this is expected and consistent with the first pass; it is a
  real, actively-researched psychological construct, just not one with disorder-level
  surveillance data. Chronic trait impatience has been associated in earlier
  cardiovascular literature with elevated hypertension/cardiovascular risk, giving it a
  real physical-health stake beyond subjective distress.

### Case grounding

No published clinical case report specifically targets "impatience" as the presenting
complaint (it is typically a secondary feature inside case reports about GAD, health
anxiety, or grief, not a primary case-study subject in the literature found).

- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A user types: "I've been waiting
  three weeks for biopsy results and I can't focus on anything, I just want to know." This
  matches Sweeny's "perfect storm" conditions precisely (high stakes + an unpleasant
  waiting state + an external process the person cannot hasten) and the "nearness effect"
  (distress intensifying, not easing, as the wait continues).
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A user types: "My partner said
  they need time to decide if they want to stay together and it's been a month — I keep
  checking my phone and I'm furious at how slow this is going." This matches the
  blame/unfairness component Sweeny identifies as central to impatience (a locus of blame
  for the delay), distinguishing it from free-floating anxiety about an unknown future.

## 23. Low mood / persistent sadness
## 24. Can't feel joy / anhedonia

**v1.0 roots (both): Death, Freedom, Isolation, Meaninglessness** (split from v0.1 #19)

These two are grouped in one write-up because the priority task for this batch — the
"all four roots" gap-fill — applies identically to both, and most of the currency/practice
research treats them as one clinical family (depression) with two distinguishable
symptom dimensions.

### Currency check (2024–2026)

Substantively active window; nothing overturns the first pass's core claims, but several
things sharpen or extend them:

- **DSM-5-TR annual updates (Sept 2024, Sept 2025)** do not change the core depressive-
  episode criteria (depressed mood / anhedonia as two independently-sufficient symptoms).
  The most notable recent DSM-5-TR-adjacent change in this territory is unrelated to
  categories 23/24 directly: prolonged grief disorder was added as its own diagnosis
  (relevant to category #2 Grief, not this batch). **Explicit finding: nothing changed in
  the diagnostic criteria underlying this category.**
- **A September 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis** ("Evaluating the efficacy of
  psychotherapeutic and pharmacological treatments for depression on anhedonia," PubMed
  40915504) found treatments produce only a **small effect on anhedonia specifically**
  (SMD = 0.26, 95% CI 0.10–0.43) — i.e., anhedonia continues to be under-treated by
  standard depression care even in the newest evidence, reinforcing (not overturning) the
  first pass's point that anhedonia needs its own dedicated treatment target.
- **Positive Affect Treatment (PAT) research has substantially matured since the first
  pass.** A May 2025 systematic review (*Australian Psychologist* / Taylor & Francis)
  identified five distinct treatment paradigms now targeting the positive-valence
  system specifically: PAT, AMP, VR-RT, ADepT, and BATA. A large PAT randomized clinical
  trial (n=98 adults with severely low positive affect) found PAT produced *greater*
  clinical improvement than a negative-affect-focused comparator therapy — meaningful
  because it's a head-to-head demonstration that anhedonia needs a mechanistically
  different intervention than standard "reduce sadness" depression treatment, not just a
  theoretical claim.
- **New transdiagnostic and mechanistic data**: a 2025 three-level meta-analysis across
  schizophrenia, depression, and subclinical populations confirms anhedonia is genuinely
  transdiagnostic (again reinforcing the first pass's caveat that "can't feel joy" is not
  depression-specific).
- **Anhedonia–suicidality link is now further specified**, not just reaffirmed: state
  (not trait) anhedonia and *recent change* in anhedonia are the components most tied to
  suicidal ideation, independent of overall depression severity (Ducasse et al.
  meta-analysis, and subsequent physician- and medical-student-sample studies). This is a
  refinement useful for the app's risk-flagging logic (see Real-world practice).
- **Anhedonia prevalence figure refined**: current sourcing puts anhedonia in 37–72% of
  MDD cases depending on measure/sample (Psychiatric Times / burden-of-illness literature),
  consistent with but more precisely bracketed than the first pass's "~90%" figure, which
  appears to be an upper-bound estimate from a subset of the literature — worth noting the
  range is wide and measure-dependent rather than a single fixed number.

**Net effect on taxonomy:** no structural change to the #23/#24 split — the 2024–2026
literature, if anything, strengthens the case for treating them as distinguishable
(different treatment protocols now exist and are being trialed specifically because they
are mechanistically different).

### Gap-fill (priority item for this batch)

**The question:** does literature connect depression/anhedonia to all four of Yalom's
existential concerns *as a unit*, or does the "all four roots" mapping remain an unsourced
design inference?

**Search conducted:** direct searches for depression/anhedonia mapped onto Yalom's four
concerns as a combined framework; direct fetch-and-read of two existential-psychotherapy
review articles (a PMC clinical review of existential issues in psychotherapy, and a
second existential-concerns paper) to check specifically for an integrated four-concern
depression model; targeted searches for each concern's *individual* link to depression
(death anxiety, loss of control/learned helplessness, isolation/loneliness,
meaninglessness) to establish whether the pieces exist separately even if no source
assembles them.

**Finding: this remains an unsourced design inference at the level of a single unified
claim — but each of the four component links is independently well-established in
separate literatures, more strongly than the first pass had evidence for.** Specifically:

1. **Death → depression**: A 2025 meta-analysis in *Nature Human Behaviour* (104 papers,
   ~24,400 cross-sectional participants) found death anxiety correlated with mental illness
   symptoms overall at r = 0.397, and *specifically* with depression, though the
   relationship is **weaker for depression than for anxiety symptoms** (r = 0.506 for
   anxiety vs. a smaller depression-specific estimate) and stronger in clinical samples
   (r = 0.580). This is real, moderate, sourced evidence for a Death–depression link, but
   it is not as strong or as central as the Death–anxiety link, which matters: **death
   anxiety is a real but secondary/moderate contributor to depression specifically**, not
   its primary driver. Terror Management Theory researchers increasingly describe death
   anxiety as "transdiagnostic," which supports inclusion as a root without claiming it is
   depression's core mechanism.
2. **Freedom (loss of control/agency) → depression**: This is the *best-established* of
   the four links, not merely plausible. Seligman's learned helplessness theory and its
   1978 reformulation (Abramson, Seligman & Teasdale) is a foundational, decades-old,
   still-standard model of depression, explicitly built on perceived loss of control over
   negative outcomes and hopelessness — this is as close to a "textbook" causal account of
   depression as exists in clinical psychology, and it maps directly onto Yalom's Freedom
   concern (agency, control, responsibility).
3. **Isolation → depression/anhedonia**: Also very well established. A meta-analysis found
   loneliness predicts new-onset depression at pooled OR = 2.33 (95% CI 1.62–3.34); a
   separate meta-analysis in older adults found social isolation associated with OR = 1.46
   for depression. For anhedonia *specifically* (not just low mood), a 2024 ecological/
   computational study ("Daily Social Isolation Maps Onto Distinctive Features of Anhedonic
   Behavior") found day-to-day social isolation directly maps onto anhedonic behavior
   patterns — a genuinely anhedonia-specific (not just depression-general) isolation
   linkage, which is new since the first pass and directly strengthens category #24's
   Isolation root specifically.
4. **Meaninglessness → depression**: Frankl's existential-vacuum concept has always framed
   depression as a potential meaning-in-life problem, and this is now backed by a December
   2025 three-level meta-analysis directly quantifying the meaning-in-life–depression
   relationship (negative correlation, consistent across multiple theoretical traditions:
   Existential Theory, Logotherapy, Cognitive Theory of Depression, Diathesis-Stress Model).

**What was not found**, despite dedicated searching and direct article review: any single
source — review, meta-analysis, or theoretical paper — that proposes depression or
anhedonia as arising from, or being usefully explained by, *all four* Yalom concerns
operating together as an integrated model. The two existential-psychotherapy review
articles fetched for this check each touch two or three of the four concerns in relation
to depression (most commonly meaninglessness, sometimes isolation), but neither assembles
all four into a unified account, and neither claims to.

**Recommendation:** Do **not** drop "all four roots" — each pillar individually has real,
often strong, sourced support (Freedom and Isolation are especially strong; Death is real
but comparatively moderate/secondary; Meaninglessness is well established via Frankl and
now meta-analytically confirmed). But the taxonomy documentation should be explicit that
**"all four roots" is a designed synthesis of four separately-sourced literatures, not a
single directly-cited claim**, and should note the relative strength ordering for the
matching/retrieval layer: **Freedom and Isolation are the strongest-evidenced roots for
this category pair; Meaninglessness is well-evidenced; Death is real but comparatively
the weakest/most secondary of the four** (useful if the retrieval layer ever needs to
weight or rank source material rather than treat all four roots as equal). This is a
stronger, more precise position than the first pass could take, because the first pass
found no component evidence at all for the individual links, whereas this pass found solid
component evidence for three of four and moderate evidence for the fourth — the composite
claim is better described as "a reasonable and now better-evidenced synthesis" than as "an
unsourced inference," even though no single source states it as a unit.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence**: WHO's most recent estimate (updated August 2025) is that **~4% of the
  world's population (~332 million people)** live with depression; prevalence is roughly
  50% higher in women than men, and rises to 5.9% in adults 70+. Global age-standardized
  incidence/prevalence/DALY rates for depression rose 11–16% from 1990 to 2021, with
  projections of continued rise to 2040 — depression is a large, real, and growing
  population health burden, confirming category 23/24 reflects an extremely common
  real-world presenting concern, not a niche one.
- **Anhedonia specifically** affects an estimated 37–72% of people with major depressive
  disorder (range across measures/samples), predicts poorer antidepressant response and a
  more severe/longer illness course, and independently predicts suicidality beyond general
  depression severity.
- **Standard-of-care treatment for low mood/depression**: CBT, behavioral activation, and
  antidepressant pharmacotherapy remain first-line; these are broadly effective for
  negative-affect (sadness) symptoms.
- **Standard-of-care treatment specifically for anhedonia** (distinguishable from generic
  depression treatment, confirming the #23/#24 split was the right call): **Positive
  Affect Treatment (PAT)** — a transdiagnostic protocol (pleasant-activity scheduling,
  attention training toward positive emotion, self/other compassion meditation) — plus
  four sibling paradigms (AMP, VR-RT, ADepT, Behavioral Activation Treatment for Anhedonia/
  BATA) now being actively trialed as of 2025. This is a mature, current, evidence-based
  answer to "what does real clinical practice actually do about anhedonia" and is a strong
  candidate description for how the app should characterize category #24 versus #23 to
  reviewers: #23 (low mood) maps toward standard CBT/behavioral-activation-adjacent
  approaches (and, downstream, philosophical material on impermanence/grief/non-attachment
  to circumstance), #24 (anhedonia) maps toward reward/motivation/positive-affect-building
  approaches (and, downstream, philosophical material on desire, contentment, and
  presence/engagement rather than on sadness per se).
- **Crisis-redirect threshold — directly relevant finding for this batch.** Anhedonia
  (specifically *state* anhedonia and *recent worsening* of anhedonia, more than general
  low mood or trait-level anhedonia) is an **independent predictor of suicidal ideation**,
  separate from overall depression severity, confirmed by a foundational meta-analysis
  (Ducasse et al., *Depression and Anxiety*) and reaffirmed in newer sample-specific
  studies (medical students, physicians). Standard digital-mental-health practice for
  crisis screening uses PHQ-9 (cutoff ≥10 for at least moderate depression) and the
  Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) for suicide-risk stratification; current
  (2025–2026) research on AI/chatbot mental-health tools explicitly evaluates them against
  C-SSRS-based simulated-risk scenarios. **Recommendation for this app's existing
  crisis-redirect logic**: because anhedonia specifically (not just sad mood) carries
  independent suicide-risk signal, category #24 (anhedonia) should not be treated as
  "lower stakes" than #23 (low mood) at the crisis-redirect layer — if anything, phrases
  signaling a *recent, sudden* loss of pleasure/interest/motivation (rather than chronic,
  stable low-grade anhedonia) deserve at least as much escalation sensitivity as sadness-
  coded language, and ideally more, per this literature. This is a concrete, actionable
  finding for wherever the app's crisis threshold is implemented.

### Case grounding

Published case material in this space is overwhelmingly pharmacological/neurobiological
(e.g., a case report of rapid antidepressant response to an opioid-modulator drug reducing
anhedonia) rather than psychosocial/presenting-complaint case studies, so no directly
transferable real case report was found for either category. Four illustrative composites,
clearly labeled:

- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case) — #23, low mood.** A user types:

## 25. Being mistreated / bullied / disrespected

**v1.0 roots: Isolation, Freedom** (v0.1 #20)

### Currency check (2024–2026)

The first pass's root mapping and coherence findings hold up; the newest material adds
legislative/policy currency and sharper intervention evidence:

- **A June 2025 systematic review in the *Journal of Business Ethics*** ("Workplace
  Mistreatment: A Systematic Review of Interventions and Future Research Agenda") examined
  40 intervention studies across incivility (10), bullying (8), lateral violence (6), and
  discrimination (3) — a substantially more current and comprehensive intervention
  evidence base than existed at the first pass, and it explicitly recommends **combined,
  multilevel strategies** (organizational policy + structural workplace change +
  relational-skills/cognitive-rehearsal training) over any single-component fix.
- **A November 2025 scoping review of systematic reviews/meta-analyses specifically for
  healthcare-worker bullying** (18 reviews synthesized) — confirms bullying-intervention
  research is active and expanding by setting (healthcare specifically), consistent with
  the first pass's general workplace-bullying grounding.
- **Legislative movement, not yet resulting in a US federal or state law**: the Healthy
  Workplace Bill campaign and a newer alternative, the Workplace Bullying Accountability
  Act (proposed by Prof. David Yamada, introduced in Massachusetts and West Virginia in
  early 2025, and New York's version S1893 passed out of committee in May 2025) show
  continuing legislative attention through 2025–2026, but **as of this writing no US state
  has enacted comprehensive workplace anti-bullying legislation** (only Puerto Rico has,
  since 2020) — a federal STOP Bullying Act was also introduced in the 119th Congress
  (2025–2026). **Explicit finding: the underlying clinical/psychological grounding is
  unchanged; what changed is policy activity, not clinical consensus** — worth noting
  because it means real-world remedies for this category's presenting concern (especially
  workplace bullying) remain limited to organizational/interpersonal-skill interventions
  rather than legal recourse in most US jurisdictions, which is relevant context for how
  the app should calibrate guidance (practical coping/boundary-setting material matters
  more than "here's your legal recourse" framing, in most cases).
- **2024 US prevalence data refresh**: the 2024 Workplace Bullying Institute survey
  (nationally representative, fielded September 2024) found **32% of US adults report
  being directly bullied at work**, another 14% witness it (46% "affected" total), and
  **65% of bullies are bosses** (vs. 21% coworkers) — a power-imbalance finding that
  directly reinforces the first pass's Freedom-root evidence (locus-of-control/learned-
  helplessness literature), since a boss-perpetrated pattern structurally limits a
  target's ability to exit or resist, which is the mechanism tying this category to loss
  of agency/control.
- **Bullying–PTSD link has a new dedicated 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis**
  ("The association of school bullying with symptoms and diagnosis of PTSD: An updated
  systematic review and meta-analysis," *ScienceDirect*, 2026), and ICD-11's inclusion of
  **complex PTSD** is now explicitly discussed in the bullying-consequences literature as a
  relevant diagnostic frame for prolonged bullying victimization (distinct from
  single-incident trauma) — a genuinely new-since-first-pass clinical nuance: chronic
  mistreatment may present as complex PTSD symptoms (identity disturbance, emotion
  dysregulation, relational difficulty) rather than classic PTSD, which is useful context
  for how severe/chronic cases in this category may look different from single-incident
  disrespect.

**Net effect on taxonomy:** no structural change. Root mapping (Isolation, Freedom)
remains, if anything, more strongly supported.

### Gap-fill

The first pass rated category #20/#25's root mapping as "unusually well-supported" with
no flagged inference gap, and this pass found nothing to weaken that. One nuance worth
adding to the matching-layer notes (not a taxonomy change): the newer complex-PTSD framing
suggests the retrieval/matching layer may want to distinguish, within the existing
"severity-weighting" recommendation the first pass already made, between (a) low-severity/
single-incident disrespect — likely well served by philosophical material on equanimity
and non-attachment to others' opinions — and (b) chronic/severe bullying with possible
complex-PTSD features — where philosophical material alone is less likely to be sufficient
and the app should consider signaling that professional support may help alongside (not
instead of) philosophical guidance, without this needing to trigger a hard crisis redirect
(mistreatment/bullying, unlike categories 23/24, was not found to carry a direct,
independent suicide-risk-marker literature comparable to anhedonia's — the risk pathway
here runs through depression/PTSD as intermediate steps rather than being a standalone
predictor the way state anhedonia is).

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence**: 32% of US adults report being directly bullied at work (2024 WBI
  survey); adding witnesses brings the "affected" figure to 46–49% (roughly 79.3 million
  US workers). This confirms the category reflects an extremely common, not niche,
  real-world experience.
- **Evidence-based intervention approaches actually in use**: per the 2025 *Journal of
  Business Ethics* review, effective interventions combine (1) organizational policy and
  reporting mechanisms, (2) structural/environmental change (e.g., staffing, supervision
  ratios in high-bullying settings like healthcare), and (3) individual-level training in
  relational skills and **cognitive rehearsal** (scripted, practiced responses to
  mistreatment scenarios — a specific, transferable technique). For the individual target
  (as opposed to organizational-level intervention), commonly used clinical approaches
  include CBT for the secondary anxiety/depression/PTSD symptoms, assertiveness/boundary-
  setting skills training, and — per the locus-of-control research — interventions aimed
  at restoring a sense of agency/internal locus of control specifically, since that is the
  documented psychological mechanism of harm.
- This category is a strong candidate for "coping/reframing" philosophical material
  (equanimity under others' behavior, non-attachment to others' opinions/judgments,
  boundary-setting grounded in self-respect) precisely because that is also the
  individual-level component of evidence-based practice (cognitive rehearsal, reframing,
  boundary skills) — i.e., the philosophy-guidance approach is plausibly complementary to,
  not a substitute for, what real-world intervention already does at the individual level.

### Case grounding

No single published case report was found that matches "being mistreated/bullied/
disrespected" as a generic presenting complaint (most case-level literature is either
population-survey-based or focused on specific severe outcomes like PTSD diagnosis
criteria), so composites only, clearly labeled:

- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case) — low-severity/single-incident.**
  A user types: "My coworker made a snide comment about my work in front of everyone and
  I can't stop replaying it." This matches the "incivility" category in the workplace-
  mistreatment typology (low-intensity, ambiguous intent, can be a single incident) —
  distinct from sustained bullying.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case) — sustained, power-imbalanced
  bullying.** A user types: "My manager has been putting me down in meetings for months
  and I've started to feel like maybe I really am as incompetent as she says, and I don't
  know how to make it stop." This matches the bullying/mobbing profile (repeated negative
  acts, power imbalance, a "helpless situation with less and less ability to avert or stop
  it") and the boss-perpetrator pattern the 2024 WBI data shows is most common (65% of
  bullying is boss-perpetrated), plus the self-blame/eroded self-esteem outcome the
  victimization literature documents.
