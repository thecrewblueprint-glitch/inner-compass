## 16. Personal identity confusion

**v1.0 roots:** Meaninglessness, Freedom. **From:** old #13 (split a). **First-pass source:** `categories-11-15.md` §13.

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- The first-pass research already cited a 2025 systematic review of reviews (Tandfonline, *Identity*, 2025) as the most current synthesis; a fresh search confirms this is still the most recent comprehensive review and nothing has superseded it. It groups post-Marcia identity models into three families — process-oriented, dual-cycle, and ethnic/cultural-identity models — which is a useful organizing update but does not change the core Marcia/Erikson mapping.
- **New in this pass:** a 2024-launched, currently-recruiting clinical trial, *Identity-Based Transdiagnostic Therapy for Young People With Anxiety and Depression* (IBTT-YOUNG, University of Barcelona, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06384196), is the first transdiagnostic psychotherapy protocol built specifically around identity work for youth anxiety/depression, compared head-to-head against the Unified Protocol. Completion is estimated late 2026. This is directly relevant real-world-practice evidence that did not exist in the original research pass and confirms identity-focused intervention is an active, funded area of clinical development, not just theory.
- No DSM/ICD diagnostic change: identity disturbance remains coded informally (Identity Problem, DSM "Other Conditions") except within Borderline Personality Disorder, where identity disturbance is a formal Criterion 3 symptom — unchanged in DSM-5-TR.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file did not flag an explicit "reasonable inference, not a direct source" claim for category 16 the way it did for old #16 (now #20). But it did note the umbrella root mapping was "defensible" rather than fully evidenced for personal identity specifically to Freedom. This pass searched directly and found real support: Marcia's own description of the *moratorium* status ties directly to Yalom's Freedom concern (agency, groundlessness, decision under uncertainty) — moratorium individuals are "in the midst of a crisis... actively exploring alternatives," reporting more anxiety than any other status because "the world for them is not, currently, a highly predictable place" (Marcia, via NSUWorks/Simply Put Psych summaries). That is a direct, sourced bridge from identity exploration to Freedom's groundlessness/agency theme, not just an inference by analogy. The Meaninglessness link remains grounded as in the first pass (unresolved self-definition as a meaning/self-authorship problem). **Conclusion: the Freedom-root link, previously more inferred than sourced, now has a direct textual bridge via Marcia's own moratorium description. Confidence upgraded.**

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence.** Identity distress (measured via Berman's Identity Distress Survey) is a real, measured phenomenon, not just theory: Berman, Weems & Petkus (2009) found 7.9% Identity Disorder / 14.3% Identity Problem rates in a high-school sample (ages 15–18); Gfellner & Cordoba (2011) found 9.7% Identity Disorder / 18.8% Identity Problem in 18–26-year-old university students across Spain and Canada. Identity diffusion is also a core, well-studied feature of Borderline Personality Disorder (prevalence 1–6% general population, 10–12% outpatient, 20–22% inpatient psychiatric settings), though most people presenting with identity confusion do not have BPD — the taxonomy category is about the much more common, non-disordered developmental struggle.
- **Treatment approaches actually in use:** Adolescent Identity Treatment (AIT/ADIT) — an integrative object-relations/family-systems/CBT model for identity pathology; narrative therapy and Narrative Identity work (including within Mentalization-Based group therapy for adolescents); and, as of 2024, the transdiagnostic IBTT protocol above. These are real, currently-practiced or currently-trialed approaches, confirming this is a recognized clinical target, not a philosophy-app invention.
- Clinically, moratorium is explicitly framed by researchers as a *normal, even healthy* developmental state, not a disorder — this matters for a philosophy-guidance app's scope: most personal-identity-confusion presentations are appropriate for reflective/philosophical support, not a clinical referral, unless identity diffusion co-occurs with self-harm, dissociation, or other BPD-spectrum features, which would need a referral.

### Case grounding

- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A 24-year-old who left a long-planned career track after a breakup describes "not knowing who I am without the plan I've had since I was 16" — high exploration, low commitment, consistent with Marcia's moratorium profile and its characteristic anxiety about an unpredictable future. Grounded in Marcia's moratorium description (NSUWorks) and identity-distress prevalence literature (Berman et al.), not a real published case.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A user who says "I've achieved everything I was supposed to — job, marriage, house — and I still don't know what I actually believe or want" describes identity *foreclosure* (commitment without exploration) reaching its limits — a pattern the identity-status literature treats as distinct from moratorium and often surfacing later in adulthood as foreclosed commitments are tested.
- No directly on-point published single-case report specific to "personal identity confusion" as a standalone presenting complaint (outside BPD-specific case literature) was located in this pass; the vignettes above are composites grounded in the cited theory/prevalence literature and are explicitly not documented cases.

## 17. Cultural or group belonging confusion

**v1.0 root:** Isolation. **From:** old #13 (split b). **First-pass source:** `categories-11-15.md` §13.

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- **New finding:** a 2026(!)-published systematic review, *Acculturation and Culturally Adapted CBT for Refugee Youth and Families* (Journal of Child & Adolescent Trauma), extends the first-pass acculturation/BII literature with a direct treatment-outcomes synthesis, concluding culturally adapted CBT helps reduce trauma symptoms and improve coping/resilience specifically via acculturation-informed adaptation. This is materially newer and more clinical-practice-focused than anything in the original research file, which cited only the acculturation/BII theory literature, not treatment-outcome reviews.
- A 2024 qualitative case study, *"Struggling like fish out of water": a qualitative case study of Chinese international students' acculturative stress in the UK* (Frontiers in Education, 2024), and 2024–2025 quantitative work on acculturative stress/loneliness among international students in China, extend the evidence base with genuinely new (post first-pass) empirical data, not just re-confirmation.
- A 2025 working paper, *Loneliness as a pathway to immigrant health decline* (Max Planck Institute for Demographic Research), is new evidence directly linking acculturative isolation to downstream physical-health decline — strengthens, rather than merely repeats, the Isolation-root mapping.
- A 2024 systematic review (ScienceDirect) on loneliness and social isolation among refugees resettled in high-income countries found prevalence rates of loneliness at 15.9–47.7% and social isolation at 9.8–61.2%, higher than general population norms — new, directly relevant prevalence data not present in the first-pass file.
- Nothing found overturns the BII/Berry acculturation-strategy framework itself; a 2025 review (MDPI) confirms psychological acculturation and BII remain theoretically related but empirically distinct constructs, consistent with the first-pass finding.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file's Isolation-root mapping for cultural/group belonging confusion was presented as reasonably well-supported (low-BII linked to "identity conflict, reduced cultural competence, impaired psychological functioning") but not explicitly pinned to Yalom's *isolation* concept by name. This pass searched directly for that bridge and found it: the 2024 refugee loneliness/social-isolation systematic review and the 2025 Max Planck loneliness-immigrant-health paper both use "loneliness" and "social isolation" as their outcome constructs directly, closing the gap between the acculturation literature (which talks about "belonging" and "cultural conflict") and Yalom's isolation root (which is about felt separateness). **Conclusion: the Isolation-root mapping is now directly evidenced by loneliness/isolation-outcome studies in acculturation populations, not just inferred from "impaired psychological functioning" language. Confidence upgraded from reasonably-inferred to well-established.**

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence.** Refugee/immigrant populations show loneliness at 15.9–47.7% and social isolation at 9.8–61.2% (2024 systematic review) — substantially elevated over general-population baselines. Newly-arrived first-generation migrant adolescents in Belgium show significantly higher depression, social, and emotional loneliness than non-newcomer peers (2024 study).
- **Treatment approaches actually in use:** "Cultural adjustment therapy" is described in current clinical-practice sources as an evidence-based approach that normalizes acculturation-stress adjustment difficulties and reduces shame/isolation; culturally adapted CBT (with demonstrated efficacy for refugee youth/families per the 2026 systematic review); trauma-informed, narrative-therapy-integrated approaches recommended by APA-aligned sources for immigrant populations; and a 2021 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMC8345801) specifically evaluating interventional programs to reduce acculturative stress and improve adjustment for international students, confirming structured interventions (not just informal support) exist and have been formally evaluated.
- This confirms cultural/group belonging confusion is a recognized, measured clinical phenomenon with its own emerging evidence-based treatment literature — appropriate for a philosophy-guidance app to address for milder/subclinical presentations, but the elevated loneliness/isolation prevalence rates suggest the app's matching logic should stay alert to severity (isolation compounding into depression or health decline) as a referral trigger.

### Case grounding

- **Real published case study:** Frontiers in Education (2024), *"Struggling like fish out of water": a qualitative case study of Chinese international students' acculturative stress in the UK* — based on 12 semi-structured interviews, found wellbeing was shaped by macro/micro acculturative stressors including academic integration, language barriers, and social integration difficulty. This is a genuine, citable case-study source (not a composite) showing the real presenting pattern: international students describing feeling neither fully part of their host culture nor able to draw on home-culture supports.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A second-generation immigrant in their 20s reports, "I'm not [heritage culture] enough for my family and not [host culture] enough for my friends — I don't feel like I fully belong anywhere," consistent with low Bicultural Identity Integration (BII) and the "caught between two cultures" pattern described in the BII literature. This is a composite built from the cited BII/acculturation literature, not a documented case.

## 18. Illness or fear about health / mortality

**v1.0 root:** Death. **From:** old #14 (unchanged). **First-pass source:** `categories-11-15.md` §14.

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- A 2024 JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis (cited in current search results) confirms CBT remains significantly effective for health-anxiety/IAD symptoms 3–12 months post-treatment — reinforcing, not changing, the standard of care already noted in the first pass.
- A 2024 comprehensive review, *Illness Anxiety Disorder: A Review of the Current Research and Future Directions* (PMC11211185), postdates the first-pass file's sources and confirms no DSM-5-TR diagnostic criteria changes; IAD remains defined by excessive illness worry with minimal/no somatic symptoms.
- New 2024–2025 death-anxiety intervention meta-analyses materially extend the first-pass file, which cited only measurement (Templer scale) literature, not treatment-outcome literature:
  - A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of psychological interventions for death anxiety in cancer patients (PMC13450554) found interventions using the Templer Death Anxiety Scale significantly reduced death anxiety vs. controls.
  - A 2024 meta-analysis of meaning-centered interventions (MCI) in advanced cancer (12 studies, 1,459 participants) found MCI improves anxiety, depression, sense of meaning, and quality of life.
  - A 2024 meta-analysis of meaning-based psychotherapy's effect on death anxiety and post-traumatic growth in cancer patients (17 studies) found positive effects on both outcomes.
  - A 2025 broader systematic review/meta-analysis (PubMed 42658918) on psychological interventions for death anxiety and distress generally (not cancer-specific) is the most current, broadest synthesis available.
- Two recent case reports (2023 IAD post-COVID; October 2025 Parkinson's-Disease-presenting-as-IAD) confirm IAD/health-anxiety case literature remains active and diagnostically nuanced (differentiating anxiety-driven presentation from genuine emergent illness).
- **Net effect:** nothing overturns the first-pass grounding; the main update is that death-anxiety intervention research (not just measurement research) is now much more developed, and CBT's efficacy for IAD specifically has fresh 2024 meta-analytic confirmation.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file did not flag an explicit "reasonable inference" caveat for category 18 — both illness anxiety and death anxiety were already well-sourced to DSM-5-TR criteria and the Templer scale literature respectively. This pass's job was to extend, not repair, that grounding, which the treatment-outcome meta-analyses above accomplish: the first pass evidenced the category as a real *construct*; this pass evidences it as a real *treatment target* with demonstrated intervention effect sizes.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence** (unchanged from first pass, still current): community 1–2-year prevalence of hypochondriasis-spectrum presentations 1.3–10% in high-income countries; IAD-specific subset (high anxiety, minimal somatic symptoms) is roughly 25% of that group.
- **Treatment approaches actually in use:** CBT remains the most widely supported treatment for IAD/health anxiety, with combination CBT+SSRI (fluoxetine) outperforming either alone in trial data; for death anxiety specifically, meaning-centered psychotherapy (originally developed for cancer populations, per Breitbart) and religiously/spiritually-integrated CBT protocols (an active 2025 clinical trial, NCT06876103, is testing an online religiously-integrated CBT intervention for death anxiety) are current, evidence-generating approaches.
- The parallel existence of a robust death-anxiety-intervention literature (largely developed in oncology/palliative contexts) is a useful real-world-grounding point: this category maps onto genuinely serious clinical populations (cancer patients, chronically ill patients) as well as the "worried well," and a philosophy-guidance app should expect a wide severity range under this single category.

### Case grounding

- **Real published case report:** Kasi et al. (2023), *A Case Report on Care-Seeking Type Illness Anxiety Disorder after COVID-19 Infection*, Case Reports in Psychiatry — a 58-year-old woman presenting with care-seeking-type IAD whose symptoms overlapped diagnostically with long-COVID, illustrating the differential-diagnosis challenge between genuine post-viral illness and anxiety-driven symptom amplification.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A user who writes "I Google every headache I get and I'm sure it's something terrible" describes the compulsive-checking, reassurance-seeking IAD pattern documented in the StatPearls/UpToDate criteria summaries — grounded in the cited diagnostic literature, not a specific real case.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A user in their 50s recently diagnosed with a chronic (non-terminal) illness writes "I can't stop thinking about the fact that I'm going to die and I don't know how to make peace with it" — an existential/philosophical death-anxiety presentation distinct from the compulsive-checking pattern above, consistent with the Templer-scale multidimensional model's "Philosophical/Temporal Fear" factor.

## 19. Financial or security fear

**v1.0 roots:** Freedom, Death. **From:** old #15 (unchanged). **First-pass source:** `categories-11-15.md` §15.

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- **Updated stress data:** the first-pass file cited APA data ("since 2007... 72% report feeling stressed about money"). Newer survey data from the 2025–2026 cycle shows the trend not only holding but intensifying: per the Motley Fool Money 2026 Financial Anxiety and Mental Health Survey (2,000 U.S. adults), 89% feel stressed about money at least occasionally, 44% rate stress as high/extremely high, 88% report financial stress entering 2026, and 77% report an actual financial setback in 2025. Inflation (38%), high daily expenses (54%), low income (46%), lack of emergency funds (39%), rising debt (35%), health care costs (34%), and job insecurity (33%) are the named drivers. This is materially newer, more granular data than the first pass had, and shows the underlying stressor has grown, not diminished.
- **New treatment-outcome evidence not in the first pass:** a randomized pilot of "Space From Money Worries," an online CBT (with mindfulness/ACT elements) intervention specifically targeting the financial-difficulties/mental-health link (Frontiers in Public Health, 2022; PMC9046654) found statistically significant improvements in depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), and perceived financial wellbeing among IAPT (UK NHS psychological therapy service) clients. This is a genuine clinical-trial-level evidence base for treating financial-anxiety presentations that the first-pass file did not have (it cited stress/scarcity/TMT theory, not intervention trials).
- **Financial Therapy Association** continues active 2024–2025 publication (Journal of Financial Therapy, Journal of Financial Counseling & Planning), including 2025 work on couple financial-stress allocation and a new Couple Financial Conflict Scale — confirming financial therapy is an actively developing applied field, not a stale niche.
- No change to the underlying scarcity-theory (Mullainathan & Shafir) or terror-management-theory (money-as-death-anxiety-buffer) mechanisms; both remain the best available causal accounts and nothing found contradicts them.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file already flagged this as its *best*-evidenced dual-root mapping and found no gaps needing repair. This pass's contribution is confirming that (a) the stress prevalence hasn't gone stale — it's higher in 2025–2026 survey data than in the pre-2024 APA figures — and (b) there is now real intervention-trial evidence (Space From Money Worries) that financial-anxiety-targeted psychological treatment measurably helps, which is a genuinely new real-world-practice grounding point, not previously present.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence:** 89% of US adults report money stress at least occasionally (2026 survey); 44% report high/extremely high financial stress; these figures track above the pre-2024 baseline the first-pass file cited.
- **Treatment approaches actually in use:** the Financial Therapy Association represents a genuine interdisciplinary clinical field bridging psychologists, marriage/family therapists, social workers, and financial planners; the Archuleta et al. Financial Anxiety Scale remains the standard validated instrument (unchanged); online CBT+ACT protocols targeting the financial-difficulty/mental-health link (Space From Money Worries) show RCT-pilot-level evidence of effectiveness; broader "financial health is behavioral health" integrated-care framing is an explicit 2025 theme in the clinical literature (Tandfonline, 2025), arguing for financial screening within standard behavioral-health intake.
- This confirms financial/security fear is not merely a lay-language complaint but a field with its own validated measurement tools, professional association, and emerging RCT evidence — appropriate for philosophy-guidance content on non-attachment/security, but a philosophy-only response would under-serve someone in acute financial crisis (job loss, imminent eviction) who needs practical financial counseling alongside any philosophical framing; worth a matching-layer note similar to the illness-anxiety referral caveat.

### Case grounding

- No specific single-patient published case report was located that is both (a) squarely about financial anxiety as the primary presenting complaint and (b) fully accessible in this pass (a Journal of Financial Therapy case vignette referencing a client with financial-management difficulties tied to past trauma was located by title but its full text was not retrievable in this pass, so it is not summarized as a verified case here).
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A user in their 30s with stable income writes "I have enough saved but I still lie awake calculating worst-case scenarios — what if I lose my job, what if the market crashes" — consistent with the scarcity-theory/TMT literature's finding that financial fear is not purely about objective resource levels but about perceived control (scarcity) and mortality-linked security-seeking (TMT), and with survey data showing high financial stress independent of income level.
- **Illustrative composite (not a documented case):** A user facing real acute financial strain ("I don't know how I'm going to make rent this month") describes the "tunneling" and narrowed-bandwidth pattern from scarcity theory — present-focused, reduced capacity for longer-term planning — a presentation where the matching layer should weight practical-groundedness alongside any philosophical material, per the 2025 "financial health is behavioral health" integration argument.

## 20. Envy / comparing myself to others

**v1.0 roots:** Isolation, Meaninglessness. **From:** old #16 (renamed Jealousy → Envy). **First-pass source:** `categories-16-20.md` §16.

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- New 2024–2025 research substantially extends the social-media/envy literature the first pass drew on:
  - A 2025 longitudinal + daily-diary study (Xiao et al., *International Journal of Psychology*, 2025) found problematic social media use *predicts* later envy, but envy did not predict later problematic use — a directional finding not present in the first-pass file's (2022-era) systematic review, which only established association, not direction.
  - A 2025 nationwide Chinese cross-sectional study ("Envy in the Feed," *European Psychiatry*, PMC13444459, data collected March 2024–May 2025) found pattern-specific social-media use predicts depression and anxiety via envy/relative deprivation, adding fresh, large-sample (1,030 students) evidence.
  - A 2025 study on body-related envy and cosmetic-surgery consideration found younger women show more body-related envy than older women from appearance-focused content exposure — a new, more specific sub-finding.
- No literature found overturns the core Smith/Kim/Parrott (1988) envy-vs-jealousy distinction; it remains the standard framework, now further reinforced by continued citation in 2024–2025 work.
- **Directly relevant new clinical-practice finding:** Robert L. Leahy's "Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Envy" (*Cognitive Therapy and Research*, June 2021, Vol. 45, Issue 3, pp. 418–427) predates the 2024–2026 window slightly but was not cited in the first-pass file at all — it is the first (and still essentially only) dedicated CBT treatment model for envy specifically, and is squarely real-world-practice-relevant, so it is added here as a gap the first pass missed rather than something that changed since.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file explicitly flagged: *"[the Isolation/Meaninglessness root mapping] is a reasonable secondary-source inference, not a direct Yalom quote, which is worth flagging as an inference rather than sourced fact."* This pass searched directly for a source closing that gap and found:
- No source directly states "envy maps to Yalom's Isolation and Meaninglessness concerns" as a single explicit claim — this specific inference **remains unconfirmed as a direct citation** and should continue to be labeled an inference.
- However, partial, real support was found for each half separately: the **Meaninglessness** link is now better evidenced via the "Envy in the Feed" (2025) and Xiao et al. (2025) findings that envy/relative deprivation predicts depression and lowered self-esteem, which is the same self-worth/meaning-deficit mechanism the first pass leaned on, now with larger and more recent samples. The **Isolation** link remains the weaker of the two: Leahy's CBT-for-envy model frames envy as arising from *social comparison* (an inherently relational, comparison-presupposes-separateness process) but Leahy's own treatment model does not explicitly invoke Yalom-style existential isolation — it treats envy through Beck's Generic Cognitive Model and Emotional Schema Therapy instead. Separately, the Existential Isolation Scale literature (Wikipedia summary of Helm et al.'s EIS/STEIM research) confirms existential isolation correlates with depression, anxiety, and reduced meaning/purpose, but no source found ties EIS scores directly to envy or social comparison specifically.
- **Conclusion: the Isolation-root mapping for envy remains a reasonable inference, not a directly sourced claim, even after this dedicated search — this should stay flagged, not treated as resolved.** The Meaninglessness-root mapping is somewhat better evidenced now via the depression/self-esteem pathway literature, though still indirect (via depression as an intermediate variable, not a direct envy→meaninglessness citation).

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Prevalence/epidemiology:** by 2025 an estimated 5.42 billion people use social networking sites globally (projected >6 billion by 2028), and multiple 2024–2025 studies link habitual social-media use to elevated envy, which in turn predicts depression/anxiety symptoms in large samples (e.g., the 1,030-student 2024–2025 Chinese cohort). This is a real, large-scale, currently-active clinical/public-health phenomenon, not a niche construct.
- **Treatment approaches actually in use:** Leahy's (2021) integrative CBT model for envy is the primary dedicated clinical protocol, combining Beck's Generic Cognitive Model with Emotional Schema Therapy. Its core techniques: (1) help clients recognize sources of envy beyond their control, (2) accept rather than suppress the experience of envy, (3) work to convert envy into admiration/emulation rather than resentment, (4) reduce avoidance/rumination, and (5) modify automatic thoughts and maladaptive assumptions about comparison and self-worth. The benign-vs-malicious-envy distinction (already in the first pass) is clinically load-bearing here: benign envy (self-improvement-motivating) needs little intervention, while malicious envy (hostility/resentment) is the actual treatment target.
- This confirms envy/social comparison is a real, recognized, if under-served, clinical target — Leahy's own paper notes "very little in the cognitive behavioral literature on the nature and treatment of envy," meaning this category sits at a genuine gap in mainstream clinical practice, which is useful context for a philosophy-guidance app: formal clinical treatment options are comparatively thin here relative to, say, illness anxiety or depression, so a well-sourced philosophical/reflective approach may be filling a real service gap rather than duplicating robust clinical infrastructure.
