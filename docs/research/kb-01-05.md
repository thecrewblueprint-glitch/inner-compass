## 1. Loneliness / feeling isolated even around people

**v1.0 mapping:** Isolation

### Currency check (2024–2026)

No change to the core theoretical picture (UCLA Loneliness Scale, Weiss's social/emotional split, Yalom's existential-isolation distinction all still stand), but several things update or sharpen it:

- **U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory** ("Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation") remains the reference policy document and is still being cited/operationalized through 2024–2025 — it frames social connection as a population-health need on par with other major risk factors, not just an individual complaint. This is a currency-relevant *scale/legitimacy* update to the first-pass research file, which didn't cite it.
- **CDC Household Pulse Survey (2024 data, published 2025)**: ~12.7% of U.S. adults report frequent loneliness; rates are substantially higher in specific subpopulations (young adults, LGBTQ+ people, some racial/ethnic minority groups).
- **College/young-adult data (2025 Healthy Minds Study)**: ~52% of college students report high loneliness; Harvard/Making Caring Common data has ~1 in 3 young adults (18–25) reporting loneliness — confirming this is not a niche presentation for a general-audience app's likely user base.
- **Existential isolation research has grown substantially 2024–2026**, moving from a mostly-theoretical construct to an actively studied one with validated new instruments:
  - A 2024 review (Averina, *Journal of Modern Foreign Psychology*) synthesizes existential-isolation research and confirms associations with depression, anxiety, accessibility of death-related thoughts, social avoidance, and hopelessness about being understood.
  - A 2024/2025 study (PubMed 39836135) specifically parses existential isolation's association with psychotherapy outcome into within-patient vs. between-patient components in naturalistic (real clinical) psychotherapy — i.e., this is now studied as a real outcome-predictor in ordinary clinical practice, not just lab studies.
  - A February 2026 paper validates a new **Self-World Existential Isolation Scale**, giving the construct a fresh, purpose-built measurement instrument (previously researchers largely repurposed general loneliness items).
  - A 2026 study in older adults (Zhao, Dewitte, et al.) found existential loneliness had the *strongest* predictive relationship with depressive symptoms among existential loneliness, existential isolation, and general loneliness — suggesting the existential-isolation reading this category's title targets may be the clinically higher-stakes one, not a secondary nuance.
- No DSM/ICD change: loneliness/isolation is still not a standalone diagnosis in either system; it remains a risk factor and transdiagnostic correlate (depression, anxiety, cardiovascular and mortality risk per the 2023 Surgeon General advisory), consistent with the first-pass file.

**Verdict:** no reversal of any first-pass claim; the existential-isolation vs. social-loneliness distinction is, if anything, more strongly evidenced now than in the original research pass, with new instruments and outcome data specific to it.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file's recommendation to have the retrieval layer "be sensitive to cues distinguishing 'I have no one' (social) vs. 'I feel alone even with people who love me' (existential)" was presented as a design recommendation, not sourced to a study that measured differential response to intervention type by loneliness subtype. Search for direct support:

- Found: multiple sources (Yalom's own clinical writing, existential-therapy literature) explicitly state that treating existential isolation with social-connection interventions is "ineffective, or even harmful" — this was already cited in the first pass and holds up.
- Found (new): the 2023/2024 outcome study (Guilford Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, and the 2024/2025 PubMed follow-up) shows existential isolation *specifically* predicts worse psychotherapy outcome and moderates a patient-therapist "match effect" — i.e., mismatching treatment approach to isolation type has a measured, not merely inferred, real-world cost in clinical data. This upgrades the first-pass file's design recommendation from "reasonable inference" to a **directly evidenced clinical finding**: mismatched intervention type for isolation subtype measurably predicts worse outcomes in naturalistic psychotherapy.
- Remaining inference: the app-specific claim that "Eastern non-duality/interconnectedness teachings map cleanly onto existential isolation" is a translation from psychology to a philosophy-matching design choice — no clinical source addresses this because it's not a clinical question. This stays a reasonable design inference, not a sourced clinical fact, and should be flagged as such for the philosophy-mapping phase.

### Real-world clinical practice grounding

- **Prevalence:** ~1 in 8 U.S. adults report frequent loneliness (CDC 2024 data); far higher in young adults and college populations (30–52% depending on measure/population). This is a genuinely common presenting concern, not a rare one.
- **Evidence-based interventions:** Masi et al.'s meta-analysis (2011, still the field's anchor citation, reaffirmed by more recent systematic reviews through 2024–2025) found that among four intervention types (improving social skills, enhancing social support, increasing opportunities for interaction, and addressing maladaptive social cognition), **interventions targeting maladaptive social cognition had the largest effect size** — i.e., CBT-style work on distorted appraisals (perceived rejection, hypervigilance to social threat, negative self-beliefs) outperforms simply increasing social contact. Recent (2025) systematic reviews of CBT specifically for loneliness in older adults reaffirm this. This is clinically important for the app: it confirms loneliness is treated less as a "go make friends" problem and more as a cognitive/appraisal problem in real practice — which is closer to what a reflective/philosophical intervention could plausibly complement than pure social-skills coaching would be.
- No specific standalone "loneliness therapy protocol" comparable to a manualized single-disorder treatment exists; loneliness is addressed as a target within CBT, and increasingly as a public-health/social-prescribing target (community referral, befriending schemes) rather than purely a clinical diagnosis — consistent with it not being a DSM/ICD category itself.

### Case grounding

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A 34-year-old married professional with a supportive spouse and an active social calendar describes feeling "completely alone" most evenings, unable to shake a sense that no one, including their partner, truly grasps their inner experience; they deny any lack of social contact and score low on measures of social-network deficits but high on existential-isolation-specific items (e.g., "I feel that no one really knows me"). This vignette is grounded in the Yalom/existential-isolation literature's core defining feature — isolation that persists "even surrounded by loving friends and family" — and in the empirical finding that existential isolation is measurably distinct from social-network loneliness (Guilford Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 2023 and follow-up studies).

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A 20-year-old college student reports withdrawing from a large, active friend group over the past semester, describing persistent loneliness despite frequent social contact, alongside low mood and academic disengagement. This composite is grounded in the 2025 Healthy Minds Study finding that roughly half of college students report high loneliness, and in Masi et al.'s finding that social-cognition distortions (not just objective isolation) commonly drive the presentation in this population.

No genuine published single-patient case report specifically labeled "loneliness" as the presenting/treated complaint was located in this search pass (loneliness case literature tends to appear as an outcome measure within broader CBT/geriatric/psychotherapy studies rather than as a discrete case report); both vignettes above are therefore composites, not documented cases.

## 2. Grief / loss of a person, relationship, or way of life

**v1.0 mapping:** Death, Freedom, Meaninglessness (broadened from Death-only in v0.1)

### Currency check (2024–2026)

Material updates since the first-pass research, which was written against the March 2022 DSM-5-TR Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) addition:

- **ICD-11 also has a PGD diagnosis** (distinct code, in the "disorders specifically associated with stress" chapter alongside PTSD/complex PTSD/adjustment disorder), with criteria that differ somewhat from DSM-5-TR's — notably a **6-month** minimum duration in ICD-11 vs. **12-month** (6 for children) in DSM-5-TR. A 2024 Frontiers in Psychiatry paper specifically compares the two systems' prevalence and diagnostic-criteria differences. The first-pass file only discussed the DSM-5-TR version — this is a genuine gap the first pass missed, not just an update.
- **New prevalence data (2024–2025) refines and lowers earlier estimates**: a 2025 scoping review and a 2025 PMC paper ("Prevalence rates of prolonged grief disorder are overestimated") both push back on earlier high estimates. Current best estimates: **~5% in probability (representative) samples vs. ~16% in non-probability (convenience/clinical) samples**; point prevalence among the bereaved generally **4.7%–6.8%** depending on criteria/algorithm used. This matters for the app: PGD is a real but minority presentation among grieving people — most bereavement, even painful bereavement, resolves without meeting disorder criteria, which is directly relevant to keeping this category framed as normal-range grief rather than implying pathology by default.
- **New digital/technology-driven grief research (2024–2026)** extends the "non-death loss" angle the first-pass file already flagged: 2026 papers on "digital severance" in relationships and on psychologically safe endings to human–AI relationships show the ambiguous-loss/non-death-loss framework is actively being extended to newer loss contexts (a currency note, not a correction).
- **A 2024 paper directly relevant to gap-fill** (see below): Ivers, "Understanding prolonged grief from an existential counseling perspective," *Journal of Counseling & Development* (2024), explicitly analyzes bereavement through Yalom's four givens, including freedom and meaninglessness — this is new, direct support for a root-mapping claim the first-pass file could only infer.

**Verdict:** no reversal; PGD prevalence estimates have been revised downward with better sampling, and the ICD-11/DSM-5-TR distinction is a documented gap in the first-pass file worth noting for accuracy.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file explicitly recommended broadening the root mapping to add Freedom and/or Meaninglessness "consistent with how ambiguous-loss and non-death-loss literature frames those experiences" — but did not cite a source that directly connects grief to Yalom's Freedom/Meaninglessness constructs by name (the ambiguous-loss and non-death-loss sources it cited are Boss's and HFA's grief frameworks, not existential-therapy frameworks specifically).

- **Found direct support:** Ivers (2024), *Journal of Counseling & Development* — analyzes prolonged/complicated grief explicitly through an existential-counseling lens, describing bereavement as capable of "shaking worldviews to the core," producing a sense of "being cast adrift" and "desperate attempts to protect oneself from existential freedom" — language that maps grief onto Freedom (groundlessness, the burden of continuing to have to choose in an altered world) in terms drawn directly from Yalom's own conceptual vocabulary, not just inferred by analogy from Boss's family-therapy framework.
- Janoff-Bulman's **shattered assumptions theory** (1992, still the standard reference, applied to grief specifically by "What's Your Grief" and others) independently supports the Meaninglessness-root connection: major loss shatters assumptions about the world's meaningfulness/fairness/predictability and the self's worth — matching the Meaninglessness root's Yalom definition ("we want life to mean something, but the universe supplies no inherent meaning") closely.
- **Verdict on this gap:** the v1.0 broadened root mapping (Death, Freedom, Meaninglessness) is now better supported than "reasonable inference" — it can be considered **well-established**, since existential-counseling literature (Ivers 2024) and trauma/loss psychology (Janoff-Bulman) both independently converge on framing major loss in Freedom- and Meaninglessness-adjacent terms, even though neither source was written to validate this specific taxonomy.

### Real-world clinical practice grounding

- **Prevalence:** PGD affects roughly 5–7% of bereaved people in representative samples (higher, up to ~16%, in clinical/convenience samples) — a real but minority subset; the large majority of people presenting with grief-related distress to this app will not meet a disorder threshold, which supports the category being framed broadly (not just clinical PGD) as the taxonomy already does.
- **Evidence-based treatments in current practice:**
  - **Complicated Grief Treatment (CGT)** and **PGD-focused CBT** are the most studied and best-supported approaches, with recent systematic reviews/meta-analyses (through 2024) reporting medium-to-large effect sizes.
  - **Narrative reconstruction therapy** — adapted from PTSD narrative work — has case-study and small-trial support specifically for PGD.
  - **Internet-delivered cognitive therapy for PGD (iCT-PG)**, a 2025/2026 therapist-assisted digital adaptation, targets loss-related memory characteristics, negative grief appraisals, social disconnection, and unhelpful coping — an active area of treatment development.
  - CBT-for-insomnia as an adjunct/entry point for PGD is being tested in single-case experimental designs (2025), reflecting how grief-related sleep disruption is being folded into standard-of-care thinking.
- This confirms grief/PGD has a genuine, currently active clinical practice literature (not just theoretical grief-stage models), appropriate as a category a philosophy-guidance app can meaningfully touch for the large non-clinical majority, while flagging that PGD-level presentations (persistent, disabling grief beyond 6–12 months) sit closer to a referral-appropriate line.

### Case grounding

**Documented case (real, published).** Boelen & Eisma et al.-adjacent literature aside, a directly citable published case: a case study of a woman with prolonged grief disorder following her father's death, treated with narrative reconstruction therapy, showing pre-/post-treatment and 3-month follow-up reductions in PGD and depression symptoms (Neimeyer et al., "Narrative reconstruction therapy for prolonged grief disorder—rationale and case study," PMC4858499).

**Documented case (real, published).** A case report of a 30-year-old man with persistent depressive-type symptoms following his father's death 24 years earlier — initially misdiagnosed as dysthymia and treated unsuccessfully with escitalopram and standard CBT — later correctly identified as Prolonged Grief Disorder and treated with a single session of Trauma Processing Therapy (memory reconsolidation, ego-state work, hypnoanalysis techniques), illustrating both how non-death-adjacent long-duration grief can be missed diagnostically and the "no closure required" long-tail pattern Boss's ambiguous-loss framework describes (Journal of Psychiatry Psychology and Behavioral Research, "When Time Doesn't Heal").

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A 45-year-old whose spouse has advanced dementia describes ongoing, unresolved grief — mourning the loss of the relationship as it was while the person is still physically present and requires care — alongside guilt about grieving someone who "hasn't died." This composite is grounded strictly in Boss's Type 2 ambiguous-loss pattern (physically present, psychologically absent) from the first-pass file's cited sources, illustrating the non-death-loss angle the app's category title explicitly covers.

## 3. General anxiety / dread about the future

**v1.0 mapping:** Freedom, Death

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- **Intolerance of Uncertainty (IU) research has deepened, not changed direction.** A large preregistered meta-analytic structural model (738 effect sizes, 115 studies, 138 samples, N=28,693) confirms IU's role as a transdiagnostic mechanism across anxiety disorders, reinforcing rather than revising the first-pass file's core claim.
- **New treatment-specificity research (2024–2025):** a 2024 pilot study directly compares **Intolerance-of-Uncertainty Therapy (IUT)** against **Metacognitive Therapy (MCT)** for GAD in primary care — both are being developed as targeted alternatives/supplements to standard CBT, and 2025 research (PMC12467993) examines emotion-regulation strategies (reappraisal vs. suppression) as an additional mechanism alongside IU. This is a genuine currency update: treatment is moving toward IU-specific protocols, not just generic CBT.
- **Terror Management Theory: a substantial 2024 meta-analysis** (referenced across TMT/death-anxiety search results) synthesizing 100+ studies found a **moderate correlation between death anxiety and a broad range of mental-health symptoms**, with clinical/anxiety-disorder populations showing stronger associations, and mortality-salience manipulations producing moderate-to-large symptom effects — a meaningful strengthening of the death-anxiety-anxiety-disorder link since the first-pass file's citations (which were mostly pre-2024 TMT reviews). A 2025 meta-analysis on insecure attachment as a predictor of death anxiety adds a new mechanism (attachment security as a moderator of TMT's "close relationships buffer death anxiety" claim).
- No DSM/ICD-5/11 diagnostic change to GAD criteria itself in this window.

**Verdict:** no reversal of the dual Freedom/Death root mapping; if anything the 2024 TMT meta-analysis is new, stronger, more directly clinical evidence for the Death-root leg specifically ("transdiagnostic role of death anxiety" language explicitly recommends incorporating existential themes into standard therapy — directly relevant to this app's premise).

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file already treated this as its best-evidenced dual-root category (both legs independently sourced, plus a source connecting the two literatures) and flagged no claim as a bare inference. No further gap-fill action was needed beyond confirming currency, which the 2024 TMT meta-analysis and the IU network-analysis/structural-model literature both do.

### Real-world clinical practice grounding

- **Prevalence:** GAD is one of the most common anxiety disorders in primary care and general population samples (widely cited lifetime prevalence in the 5–9% range in Western epidemiological samples; this figure is unchanged in the literature reviewed and wasn't re-derived here since it wasn't flagged as needing an update).
- **Evidence-based treatments in current practice:**
  - **CBT remains first-line**, including network meta-analyses (2025, PMC12162829) comparing delivery formats (individual, group, guided self-help) for GAD.
  - **Intolerance-of-Uncertainty Therapy (IUT)** and **Metacognitive Therapy (MCT)** are actively being tested as IU-targeted alternatives (2024 pilot data).
  - **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)** has a solid and growing evidence base specifically for GAD, including in older adults (large, equivalent improvements to cognitive therapy in a head-to-head RCT) and for repetitive negative thinking/worry specifically (multiple-baseline studies) — ACT's acceptance-of-uncertainty and values-based framing is conceptually close to what a philosophy-guidance approach could plausibly complement.
  - Worry-specific interventions (worry exposure, problem-solving training, cognitive avoidance reduction) remain standard components per the IU Model of GAD.
- This is a well-established, heavily practiced clinical area with strong current standard-of-care documentation — good grounding for the category as a real, common, well-studied clinical phenomenon.

### Case grounding

**Documented case (real, published).** A published clinical case (*ScienceDirect*, "A Clinical Case of Generalized Anxiety Disorder") and its 2025 update (WJARR, "A clinical case of generalized anxiety disorder") — case literature describing a 38-year-old man with longstanding excessive, uncontrollable worry about daily matters (family, health, work), with chronic restlessness, irritability, muscle tension, concentration difficulty, and sleep disturbance, consistent with core GAD diagnostic features.

**Documented case (real, published).** A published case of a 28-year-old patient presenting with excessive, uncontrollable worry, fear of "losing his mind" from worrying, apprehension about workplace inadequacy, poor concentration, and fatigue — illustrating the "dread about the future" framing (fear that worry itself signals loss of control) central to the IU literature (*International Journal of Indian Psychology* case study PDF).

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A 27-year-old in a stable job and relationship reports constant, hard-to-turn-off worry about hypothetical future scenarios (job loss, illness, relationship breakdown) despite no current evidence any of them is likely, alongside muscle tension and difficulty sleeping — grounded in the IU literature's core finding that people high in IU find uncertainty itself threatening "regardless of the actual probability of a negative event."

## 4. Anger

**v1.0 mapping:** Freedom (split from v0.1 #4 "Anger/conflict"; conflict half now category 5, below)

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- **ICD-11 formally added Intermittent Explosive Disorder (IED)** as a standalone diagnosis under Impulse Control Disorders (code 6C73) when it came into effect in 2022 — this is new relative to the first-pass file, which discussed the DSM-5 diagnostic gap ("no diagnostic category where anger is the presenting issue" comparable to anxiety disorders) but didn't address ICD-11's parallel move. ICD-11's IED criteria are broadly similar to DSM-5-TR's but slightly less specific on frequency thresholds, and it explicitly distinguishes IED from oppositional defiant disorder and conduct-dissocial disorder. This is a genuine gap-fill/currency addition: the "DSM doesn't have a category" framing in the first-pass file is DSM-specific and doesn't extend to ICD-11.
- **A comprehensive 2024/2025 meta-analysis of IED treatment** (PMC11740934) synthesizes both psychological and pharmacological treatment evidence from case studies and RCTs — a genuine update, since the first-pass file's anger sources predate this and focused on Deffenbacher's CBT model without IED-specific outcome synthesis.
- No material change to Deffenbacher's model or the "anger iceberg" secondary-emotion framing; both remain the standard clinical reference.
- New mechanism research (2024–2025, e.g. "The effects of subjective loss of control on risk-taking behavior: the mediating role of anger") directly supports — with data, not just theory — that anger functions as a response to perceived loss of control, a finding cross-culturally replicated in German and Chinese samples.

**Verdict:** no reversal; the DSM-vs-ICD point is a real gap the first-pass file should have but didn't address, now filled.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file's Freedom-root justification for anger rested on "the taxonomy's own framing: responsibility/control over one's reaction" — explicitly flagged there as fitting "reasonably" rather than as a directly-sourced clinical claim connecting anger to Yalom's Freedom construct by name.

- **Found direct support:** the subjective-loss-of-control → anger research (PubMed 26217244 and its PMC full text) provides an actual empirical (not merely theoretical) link between perceived loss of control/agency and anger as a response — anger functioning as an attempt to "reassert agency in a situation that feels threatening." This is real data support for connecting anger to a control/agency mechanism, which is conceptually close to Yalom's Freedom given (responsibility, groundlessness, the burden of having to choose/act).
- However, **no source found explicitly frames anger through Yalom's existential-Freedom vocabulary** the way the Ivers (2024) paper did for grief. Search specifically for "Yalom anger freedom" and "four givens anger" returned only general existential-therapy material connecting *freedom/responsibility* as a given, and *anger* only indirectly, as a byproduct of existential anxiety under that given — not a source that names anger and Yalom's Freedom construct together.
- **Verdict on this gap:** upgraded from a bare design inference to a **partially evidenced inference** — the underlying psychological mechanism (anger ↔ perceived loss of control/agency) is now directly data-supported, but the specific mapping onto Yalom's existential-Freedom vocabulary (as opposed to the more general clinical construct of "locus of control") remains a reasonable inference, not a directly sourced clinical claim. This should stay flagged, not silently upgraded to "well-established."

### Real-world clinical practice grounding

- **Prevalence:** national data reviews (2024) estimate roughly 7–11% of U.S. adults experience clinically significant anger problems; IED specifically has a 7.3% lifetime prevalence per the National Comorbidity Survey Replication (~16 million Americans), yet under 15% of people with serious anger issues have ever sought professional help — a large treatment gap, relevant context for a self-guided app touching this space (real unmet need, but also real risk that some presentations exceed what self-guided philosophy content should address).
- **Evidence-based treatments in current practice:**
  - **CBT is the dominant, most disseminated treatment** for both anger and aggression; a landmark meta-analysis found an effect size of 0.70 (moderate-to-large) for CBT-based anger treatment, with roughly 75% of completers showing measurable improvement.
  - **SAMHSA's manualized 12-session CBT anger-management protocol** is widely used in U.S. clinical and correctional settings.
  - For **IED specifically**, the 2024/2025 meta-analysis found both psychological (predominantly CBT: cognitive restructuring, relaxation training, problem-solving, assertiveness/communication skills) and pharmacological treatment (antidepressants/mood stabilizers showed moderate-to-marked reduction in aggressive impulses in ~60% of monotherapy recipients in the foundational case series) have evidence support, with combined approaches often preferred for more severe presentations.
  - "Anger iceberg" framing (anger as a secondary emotion over hurt/fear/sadness/shame) remains a popular and clinically used psychoeducational tool, though it is more a clinical heuristic than a formally validated model.
- IED comorbidity data (93% mood disorders, 48% substance use disorders, 48% anxiety disorders, 44% another impulse-control disorder in the classic case series) is a useful practical marker: severe/recurrent anger presentations are very often not "pure" anger but comorbid with other conditions — relevant for the app's implicit scope boundary (mild-to-moderate anger as a mood/reaction vs. IED-level presentations needing referral).

### Case grounding

**Documented case (real, published).** A published case report (*Industrial Psychiatry Journal*, "A case of intermittent explosive disorder") describing a serving soldier presenting with sudden, disproportionate, poorly-directed explosive outbursts of anger and violence, followed by a sense of gratification/relief, with recurrent episodes resulting in assault or property destruction, tension/arousal build-up before the act, and no subsequent guilt or remorse — a textbook IED presentation.

**Documented case (real, published).** DSM-IV intermittent explosive disorder: a report of 27 cases (PubMed 9590677) — a case series (not a single case, but genuinely documented, not composite) finding all subjects described aggressive impulses prior to aggressive acts, most associated their episodes with mood/energy changes, and high rates of comorbid mood, substance-use, and other impulse-control disorders — directly grounding the comorbidity pattern above.

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A 31-year-old reports frequent flashes of intense anger triggered by minor frustrations (traffic, a colleague's tone, a partner's forgetfulness), followed by regret, without meeting the frequency/severity threshold for an IED diagnosis — describing the anger as "coming out of nowhere" and only later recognizing an underlying feeling of being disrespected or powerless in the triggering moment. This composite is grounded strictly in the "anger iceberg" secondary-emotion framing and the loss-of-control/agency mechanism research cited above, deliberately pitched below the clinical IED threshold to represent the non-clinical majority of anger presentations this app is more likely to actually see.

## 5. Ongoing conflict with someone

**v1.0 mapping:** Freedom, Isolation (split from v0.1 #4 "Anger/conflict"; anger half is category 4, above)

### Currency check (2024–2026)

- Gottman's Four Horsemen model (criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling) remains the dominant clinical reference; no contradicting or materially revising 2024–2026 research was found in this search pass. Several 2025 blog/clinical-guide sources reaffirm rather than revise it — flagged in the first-pass file's terms, this is genuinely "**no material updates found as of 2026-09-18**" for the core model.
- **A 2024 pilot RCT** (published in *The Family Journal*) found Gottman Method Couples Therapy outperformed treatment-as-usual specifically for couples recovering from infidelity — a new, direct RCT data point (the first-pass file's Gottman citations were mostly descriptive/clinical-guide sources, not RCT outcome data).
- **New research (2022–2025) reconceptualizes loneliness as inherently relational**, not just an individual trait — finding loneliness can be "contagious" within couples/social networks and is bidirectionally linked with relationship conflict, satisfaction, commitment, and trust. This is directly relevant new evidence for the Isolation-root question below.
- **Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT, Sue Johnson)** was not discussed at all in the first-pass file's category-4 research, despite being, per multiple 2024–2025 sources, "one of the best validated couples interventions in North America" with ~35–40 years of accumulating research — this is a genuine gap in real-world-practice coverage the first-pass file missed (it only cited Gottman), addressed below.

**Verdict:** core model unchanged; new RCT-level Gottman outcome data and newly-found EFT evidence base fill practice-grounding gaps the first pass didn't cover.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass file explicitly flagged the Isolation-root mapping as a design inference: "Gottman's research is fundamentally about relational bonds fraying, which is closer to Isolation" — a re-framing by the research author, not a direct quote from Gottman's own work using Yalom's vocabulary.

- **Found direct support:** the "loneliness is inherently relational" research line (2022–2026, including a study specifically on relationship conflict and momentary loneliness/affect in older couples' daily lives, and a Gottman Institute clinical piece titled "I'm Lonely in My Relationship") provides real evidence that **ongoing relational conflict and loneliness/isolation are empirically linked as a real clinical phenomenon**, not just conceptually adjacent. Specifically: higher conflict frequency both emanates from and creates conditions that erode connection; chronic conflict impairs partners' ability to return to emotional baseline; loneliness can be "contagious" within a couple. This is meaningfully stronger support than the first-pass file had — it moves the Isolation-root claim from "the research author's re-framing of Gottman" to **a directly evidenced empirical link between chronic conflict and relational loneliness/isolation** in its own right (independent of Gottman specifically).
- **Verdict on this gap:** upgraded from "reasonable inference" to **reasonably well-supported** (not quite "well-established" in the sense of a single canonical source stating "conflict maps to Isolation," but now backed by an independent, converging empirical literature rather than just one research author's interpretive leap).
- The **Freedom** leg of this category's root mapping was not separately argued in the first-pass file beyond "fits the anger-as-emotion-regulation half" (i.e., borrowed from category 4's reasoning) — no new source found that directly grounds ongoing interpersonal conflict in Yalom's Freedom construct specifically. This remains an **inference carried over from the anger category** rather than an independently sourced claim for conflict specifically, and should be flagged as such (weaker support for Freedom-as-primary-root on the conflict side than for Isolation).

### Real-world clinical practice grounding

- **Prevalence:** ongoing relationship conflict is an extremely common presenting issue in couples/family therapy caseloads generally (this wasn't separately quantified in this pass, since no epidemiological "prevalence of ongoing conflict" figure exists the way disorder-prevalence figures do — conflict is dimensional/relational, not a diagnosable condition, consistent with the first-pass file's framing).
- **Evidence-based treatments in current practice:**
  - **Gottman Method Couples Therapy** — Sound Relationship House framework, teaches conflict-management, friendship-building, and shared-meaning skills; Gottman Institute reports ~75% of completing couples show improved relationship satisfaction, with a 2024 pilot RCT showing superiority over treatment-as-usual for infidelity recovery specifically.
  - **Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)** — attachment-based, reframes conflict as distress in the underlying attachment bond rather than a content dispute; multiple RCTs (including for infertile couples with marital conflict) support efficacy; considered alongside Gottman Method one of the two dominant evidence-based couples modalities in current practice.
  - Both approaches share a key clinical insight directly relevant to this app's angle: **conflict itself is not what predicts relational breakdown — its frequency, whether repair attempts are made and accepted, and whether it's "unrepaired" over time is what matters** (Gottman), and **conflict is often a surface expression of an underlying attachment/connection rupture** (EFT) — both point toward this category being fundamentally about rupture-and-repair of connection, supporting the Isolation-root gap-fill finding above.
- This is a well-established clinical practice area (couples therapy is one of the most mature applied-psychology fields) with strong current standard-of-care documentation.

### Case grounding

No genuine single-patient published case report specific to "ongoing conflict with someone" as a standalone presenting complaint (outside couples-therapy trial write-ups, which report aggregate outcomes rather than individual case narratives) was located in this search pass. Both vignettes below are therefore composites.

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A couple in their late 30s, married eight years, seeks help because arguments about household responsibilities have become a recurring pattern: one partner raises the issue with growing frustration (criticism), the other responds by pointing out the first partner's own shortcomings (defensiveness) or shutting down and leaving the room (stonewalling); both describe feeling more like "roommates" than partners and increasingly lonely within the relationship despite living together. This composite is grounded strictly in Gottman's Four Horsemen framework and the "loneliness is contagious within couples" research cited above.

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** An adult describes an unresolved years-long conflict with a sibling that flares up at every family gathering — neither person is willing to be the first to repair the rupture, and the adult describes feeling isolated within the wider family as a result, avoiding gatherings altogether. This composite is grounded in the Gottman "repair attempt" literature (chronic conflict distinguished from discrete disagreement specifically by absence of accepted repair) and the relational-isolation research above, extended to a non-romantic relationship to reflect that Gottman's model, while developed on couples, is commonly applied clinically to other ongoing relationships (a generalization worth flagging: the primary evidence base is couples-specific, not validated to the same degree for sibling/friend/coworker conflict).
