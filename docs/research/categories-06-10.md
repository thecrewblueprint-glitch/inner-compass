## 6. Rejection / heartbreak

**Maps to (draft):** Isolation, Meaninglessness

**What the literature says.** Romantic/social rejection is one of the most heavily studied "everyday" psychological injuries. Neuroimaging work (Fisher et al., published in *Journal of Neurophysiology*) shows rejection in love activates brain regions tied to physical pain (anterior cingulate cortex, anterior insula) as well as reward, motivation, and addiction-craving circuitry (ventral tegmental area, ventral striatum) — i.e., heartbreak is processed partly like a substance-withdrawal state, not just a sad mood. APA's own summary ("The pain of social rejection," APA Monitor) confirms the pain-overlap finding and notes rejection can trigger genuine depressive episodes. Separately, there is real (if narrow) cardiological literature on "broken heart syndrome" (takotsubo cardiomyopathy) — most documented cases are triggered by bereavement/grief rather than romantic breakup specifically, so that physiological finding is more squarely a Death/grief phenomenon than a heartbreak one; it's a useful nuance but not strong evidence for restructuring this category.

Ostracism/rejection researcher Kipling Williams's body of work treats *ostracism*, *exclusion*, and *rejection* as related but empirically distinguishable constructs — rejection is an active "I don't want you," ostracism is being ignored/invisible, and both are distinct from the *loneliness* that can result from either. This matters for taxonomy design: category 1 ("Loneliness / feeling isolated even around people") is the chronic/ambient state; category 6 is the acute precipitating event (a specific rejection or breakup). They are causally linked but not the same presenting complaint — someone typing "he broke up with me" is describing an event, not ambient loneliness.

**Coherence as a single category.** Reasonably coherent as an acute-event category, but it currently bundles two things that clinical/research framing treats somewhat differently: (a) romantic rejection/heartbreak (breakup, unrequited love — reward-circuitry/craving-dominant, per Fisher et al.) and (b) broader social rejection (being excluded, fired, "the friend group dropped me" — ostracism-dominant, per Williams). Both are well-evidenced but have slightly different phenomenology (craving/obsessive longing vs. wounded self-esteem/invisibility). For a v0.1 taxonomy this is an acceptable bundle since both root to Isolation, but the guidance-matching layer should be aware a "romantic heartbreak" query and a "friend group excluded me" query may want different source passages even within the same category.

**Redundancy check.** Overlaps meaningfully with category 1 (Loneliness) as a downstream state, and with category 10 (Betrayal) when the rejection involves infidelity or someone breaking a commitment rather than a "clean" breakup — see category 10 below for the recommended dividing line.

**Root mapping.** Isolation is well supported (ostracism thwarts belonging need — Williams). Meaninglessness is also defensible: rejection research (APA Monitor; Fisher et al.) documents identity disruption and loss of an imagined future/life narrative, which is a meaning-structure injury, not just a bond injury. Keep both roots.

## 7. Feeling stuck / can't decide

**Maps to (draft):** Freedom

**What the literature says.** This category maps cleanly onto two converging bodies of work. First, the classic choice-overload/decision literature: Iyengar & Lepper's jam-study finding that people acted more (and were more satisfied) with 6 options than 24 is the foundational citation (widely cited via Barry Schwartz's *The Paradox of Choice*). Second, and more directly relevant to a philosophy-matching app, Yalom's own account of Freedom as an existential given: he writes that "freedom... often paralyzes you and you end up doing nothing," describes "responsibility-voiding defenses" (compulsivity, displacement of responsibility onto others, denial of responsibility, "decisional pathology"), and frames responsibility as "authorship of one's own self, destiny, life predicament." This is a strong, direct textual match — arguably category 7 is the single cleanest 1:1 mapping onto Yalom's Freedom root in the whole taxonomy.

Clinically, "decision paralysis" / "analysis paralysis" is discussed by sources like Cleveland Clinic and Psychology Today (2026) as cognitive overload plus identity/threat appraisal — i.e., when a choice is tied to self-concept or others' expectations, the nervous system treats it as a threat, which is functionally very close to Yalom's "freedom anxiety."

**Coherence as a single category.** Coherent, but the literature actually names two different mechanisms that get folded into "feeling stuck": (1) *choice overload* (too many options, comparison fatigue — a cognitive/environmental problem, per Iyengar/Lepper/Schwartz) and (2) *decisional avoidance from identity threat / fear of being wrong* (an emotional-regulation and existential-responsibility problem, per Yalom and the "analysis paralysis" clinical sources). These aren't identical: someone choosing between 12 job offers has a different problem from someone unable to leave a relationship they know is wrong. Given the category maps to a single root (Freedom) either way, a split is not strictly necessary for the taxonomy's purposes, but the guidance layer should keep both flavors of source material (choice-architecture material AND responsibility/groundlessness material) rather than assuming one covers both.

**Redundancy check.** No strong overlap with adjacent categories 6/8/9/10. Mild adjacency to category 12 (Overwhelm) and category 18 (Impatience) — both also Freedom-rooted — but "can't decide" is specifically about an unmade choice, which is distinct enough to keep separate.

**Root mapping.** Freedom is very well supported — arguably the best-evidenced single-root mapping among all 20 categories, given Yalom uses "the problem of choice" as his own worked example of the Freedom concern.

## 8. Fear of failure or of a specific outcome

**Maps to (draft):** Freedom, Death

**What the literature says.** "Fear of failure" has a specific clinical name in the literature: **atychiphobia** (from Greek *atyches*, "unfortunate"), covered by Cleveland Clinic, Medical News Today, Talkspace, and academic sources (EBSCO Research Starters; a 2024 ResearchGate study on atychiphobia and student performance). It's characterized as fear rooted in self-worth beliefs ("this fear is not just about failing at a task, but about what failing says about them as a person" — Talkspace/Psychologs), often traced (per Penn State-sourced material cited across several of these) to demeaning caregivers, early embarrassing failures, or a major failure the person was ill-equipped to cope with. This is a well-populated clinical literature, not a fringe construct.

The Death-root mapping is more indirect but has real support: Terror Management Theory (Wikipedia; death-anxiety review in *Clinical Psychology Review*, ScienceDirect) treats loss of control as a trigger for death-anxiety-adjacent ego threat, and separate research on ego-integrity (Erikson-adjacent) finds that viewing one's life as "a series of failed and missed opportunities" increases death anxiety in later life. So "fear of failure" and "fear of death" are linked via ego-threat/loss-of-control mechanisms, but it's a theoretical bridge (TMT), not a direct clinical equivalence — atychiphobia sources themselves frame it primarily as a self-esteem/shame phobia, not a mortality fear.

**Coherence as a single category.** The category as named actually bundles two distinguishable things: (1) *fear of failure* proper (atychiphobia — chronic, self-worth-based, avoidance/procrastination-driving) and (2) "fear of a specific outcome," which is really closer to *anticipatory anxiety about an uncertain future event* (a health result, a decision's consequence, a exam/interview outcome). The second is arguably better covered by category 3 (General anxiety/dread about the future) than by this one. As currently named, this category risks being a catch-all for "any future-oriented fear," which dilutes its usefulness for retrieval matching.

**Redundancy check.** Meaningful overlap with category 3 (General anxiety/dread about the future) on the "specific outcome" half, and with category 15 (Financial/security fear) when the feared outcome is financial. Recommend narrowing this category to fear-of-failure specifically (atychiphobia-pattern: self-worth tied to performance/outcome, avoidance behavior) and letting category 3 absorb generic "worried about an upcoming outcome" queries that aren't about personal adequacy.

**Root mapping.** Freedom is solid (failure fear is fundamentally about the burden of choosing to act under uncertainty with no guarantee — a groundlessness problem). Death is defensible via the TMT/ego-threat bridge but weaker/more indirect than the Freedom link; keep it, but the guidance layer shouldn't expect atychiphobia sources to talk about mortality directly — the Death-root material for this category should lean on impermanence/non-attachment-to-outcome teachings rather than literal death framing.

## 9. Substance use / compulsive coping

**Maps to (draft):** Death, Meaninglessness

**What the literature says.** This category needs the most restructuring of the five. Three separate, well-evidenced bodies of research are relevant:

1. **DSM-5 substance use disorder.** APA's DSM-5 replaced "abuse/dependence" with a single *substance use disorder* diagnosis, staged mild/moderate/severe, on four symptom clusters: impaired control, social impairment, risky use, pharmacological criteria (tolerance/withdrawal).
2. **Behavioral/process addiction.** DSM-5 currently recognizes only **gambling disorder** as a non-substance behavioral addiction (citing insufficient research to formally add others like compulsive shopping, sex, or internet use), but the neuroscience is well documented as overlapping: gambling and substance addiction share dysregulation of the mesolimbic dopamine reward circuit (VTA, nucleus accumbens), and comorbidity is high — roughly 1-in-2 to 1-in-3 people with gambling disorder also have a substance use disorder (ScienceDirect; multiple clinical sources).
3. **Why people use — the self-medication hypothesis.** Khantzian's self-medication hypothesis (1985, updated 1997; still actively cited, e.g. PMC 2015 reconsideration) is the most directly relevant framework for a "compulsive coping" category: it holds that substance use is often an attempt to regulate painful affect states, not primarily pleasure-seeking, and that vulnerability centers on deficits in self-regulation of affect, self-esteem, relationships, and self-care.

Frankl's existential-vacuum concept (logotherapy) directly supports the Meaninglessness mapping: Frankl explicitly names addiction, alongside aggression and depression, as one leg of a "mass neurotic triad" that emerges from chronic meaninglessness, and describes addictive behavior as an attempt to fill the vacuum left by a frustrated "will to meaning." This is a strong, named, citable link — arguably the single best-evidenced root-mapping justification among all five categories reviewed here.

**Coherence as a single category — recommend splitting.** "Substance use" (a DSM-5-defined clinical entity with pharmacological criteria) and "compulsive coping" more broadly (gambling, compulsive shopping/eating/internet use, i.e., behavioral/process addictions) are related but formally distinct in the literature — DSM-5 itself only certifies gambling disorder as clinically equivalent to substance addiction, explicitly declining to certify the others for lack of evidence. Bundling them risks the guidance-matching layer treating "I can't stop drinking" and "I can't stop online shopping" identically when the evidence base, physiological stakes (withdrawal, overdose risk), and appropriate crisis-resource thresholds differ. Recommend splitting into (a) **Substance use** (alcohol/drugs, DSM-5 SUD-aligned) and (b) **Compulsive/behavioral coping** (gambling, compulsive shopping, overeating, doomscrolling/internet use, etc.), both still mapped to Death + Meaninglessness, sharing the Khantzian self-medication and Frankl existential-vacuum framing, but allowing the philosophy content and any safety-resource thresholds to differ.

**Redundancy check.** Low overlap with the other four categories in this batch. Some overlap with category 12 (Overwhelm) and category 19 (Low mood) as common triggers for self-medication, but those are precipitants, not the same presenting complaint.

**Root mapping.** Meaningfulness/Meaninglessness is very strongly supported (Frankl, direct and named). Death is more debatable as currently framed — it likely reflects mortality/self-destruction risk (overdose, health decline) rather than a Yalom-style "impermanence" theme; this is defensible but worth flagging: unlike categories 8 or 14, the literature here doesn't frame substance use primarily as an existential-death-awareness issue, it frames it as an affect-regulation/meaning issue. Keep Death in the mapping (physical mortality risk from substances is real and citable via SUD severity literature) but expect the strongest, most literature-backed guidance content to come from the Meaninglessness side.

## 10. Betrayal / broken trust

**Maps to (draft):** Isolation

**What the literature says.** The dominant citable framework here is Jennifer Freyd's **betrayal trauma theory** (coined 1991/1994, University of Oregon Freyd Dynamics Lab). BTT's original clinical scope is specifically betrayal by someone a person *depends on for survival* — the paradigm case is childhood abuse by a caregiver — and it explains phenomena like betrayal blindness and dissociation as adaptive responses to depending on one's betrayer. Freyd's related concepts (institutional betrayal, DARVO, institutional courage) extend this to organizations (e.g., healthcare institutions failing to protect a patient). Research under this framework finds betrayal trauma survivors show measurable harm and elevated risk of *misplaced trust* in future relationships (impaired trust calibration, not just "less trust").

Separately, there's a large adjacent literature on **romantic betrayal/infidelity specifically** (GoodTherapy, Psychology Today, and academic sources like the Fitness "Betrayal, Rejection, Revenge, and Forgiveness" chapter) that treats betrayal as "a trusted partner intentionally violating trust," with infidelity as the single most-cited form, alongside lying, revealing secrets, and failure to provide help. This romantic/interpersonal betrayal literature is related to but distinct from Freyd's dependency-based betrayal trauma — Freyd's theory is strongest for high-stakes dependency relationships (caregiver, institution one relies on for safety), while everyday relational betrayal (a friend's lie, a partner's affair between adults with exit options) is a real but lower-intensity phenomenon covered by a different, more social-psychological literature.

**Coherence as a single category.** Reasonably coherent as named, but the taxonomy should be precise about scope: "betrayal / broken trust" as commonly typed by a user will mostly mean interpersonal betrayal (infidelity, a friend's lie, a business partner's dishonesty) rather than the caregiver-dependency betrayal trauma Freyd's original theory targets. Both are legitimate and both cite to real research, but the philosophy-matching content should not assume every "betrayal" query is a high-stakes dependency trauma — most will be closer to the Fitness/relationship-betrayal literature. No split needed, but a nuance note is warranted (see below).

**Redundancy check.** This is the category with the clearest overlap risk in the batch: infidelity-driven heartbreak could be tagged either "betrayal" (10) or "rejection/heartbreak" (6). The literature suggests a workable dividing line: category 6 fits when the relationship *ended* (loss is the primary frame — grief/isolation from an ended bond); category 10 fits when *trust itself* was violated, especially if the relationship continues or the violation (not the ending) is the presenting issue — e.g., "I found out my partner lied for years" even if they're still together. Also worth flagging: category 4 ("Anger / ongoing conflict with someone") and category 20 ("Being mistreated / bullied / disrespected") both can shade into betrayal when the conflict involves a trust violation rather than a values clash — no merge recommended, but the boundary should be documented for whoever builds the matching logic.

**Root mapping.** Isolation is well supported — betrayal trauma research frames the core injury as a rupture in dependency/attachment and (per Freyd) in *safety in relying on others*, which is squarely Yalom's Isolation territory. No strong evidence found to add a second root, though a case could be made for a light secondary Meaninglessness mapping (betrayal often shatters a person's narrative/assumptive-world about the relationship or the trusted party) — this is more of a soft suggestion than something the literature strongly forces; recommend keeping single-root as drafted unless the philosophy-mapping stage finds it needs the extra hook.
