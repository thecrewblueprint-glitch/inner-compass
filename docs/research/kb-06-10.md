## 6. Shame / feeling fundamentally not okay

**v1.0 mapping:** Meaninglessness, Isolation (unchanged from v0.1 #5)

### Currency check

No material change to the core construct or its DSM/ICD status: shame remains a cross-cutting affective phenomenon, not a standalone diagnosis, in both DSM-5-TR (no 2024 or September 2025 update supplement entries add or alter a shame diagnosis) and current ICD-11. Three genuine 2024–2026 developments refine, without overturning, the first-pass findings:

1. **New neuroimaging support for the cross-cultural caveat.** A 2025 SDM (seed-based d mapping) meta-analysis, "Mapping universal and culturally specific moral brain: neural correlates in shame and guilt across East Asian and Western contexts" (*ScienceDirect*, 2025), gives the first-pass's cross-cultural caveat actual neuroimaging backing rather than only behavioral/survey backing — it finds both shared and culture-specific neural correlates for shame vs. guilt processing across East Asian and Western samples.
2. **A fresh, non-Chinese collectivist data point.** Kim & Turner, "A grounded theory study on Korean doctoral students' shame experiences in the U.S.: Exploring the role of cultural self-construals" (*Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology*/SAGE, 2025) extends the externalized/face-based shame finding beyond the China-focused literature the first-pass cited, into a Confucian-collectivist, cross-national (Korean-in-U.S.) sample — directly relevant to an app sourcing Eastern teachings, since it shows the externalized-shame pattern travels with the person across cultural contexts, not just within-country.
3. **A 2025 systematic review of the broader self-conscious-emotion literature** ("Understanding shame, guilt, embarrassment and pride: a systematic review of self-conscious emotions," *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2025) searched peer-reviewed literature 2000–2024 and confirms persistent methodological gaps and cross-cultural variability in how shame is elicited and expressed — i.e., the field itself still flags this as unsettled, which argues for keeping the first-pass's caution rather than hardening it into a fixed cross-cultural rule.

No changes to root mapping or category name are indicated. **No material updates found that would change the Isolation/Meaninglessness root mapping itself as of September 2026** — the new work strengthens the existing cross-cultural caveat rather than altering the core Brown-based shame/guilt distinction.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass Shame section (`categories-01-05.md` §5) does not contain an explicit "reasonable inference" flag the way categories 8 and 9 (below) do — its claims (Brown's "I am bad" vs. "I did something bad" distinction; AVPD/social-anxiety shame overlap; the cross-cultural externalized/internalized split) are each already tied to a specific cited source. The one place worth stress-testing is **how much weight Brown's work itself can bear**: her Shame Resilience Theory rests on one peer-reviewed grounded-theory study (Brown, 2006, *Families in Society*, n=215 women) plus a large body of non-peer-reviewed trade-book synthesis. Searching specifically for independent replication or critique surfaced no direct empirical replication of the original 2006 grounded-theory study and no substantive peer-reviewed critique disputing the "I am bad" vs. "I did something bad" distinction itself (that distinction is separately and independently supported by Tangney's guilt/shame research, cited in the taxonomy doc for category 21). The fair characterization: **the shame/guilt distinction is well-established and independently corroborated (Tangney), but Shame Resilience Theory specifically as a named clinical model rests on a single primary qualitative study** — this was implicit in the first-pass but is worth stating plainly rather than leaving unstated.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT)** is the most directly shame-targeted evidence-based approach. A 2024 systematic review, "The Effectiveness of Compassion Focused Therapy for the Three Flows of Compassion, Self-Criticism, and Shame in Clinical Populations" (PMC, 2024), and an earlier meta-analysis (Vidal et al., *British Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 2023) both find consistent reductions in self-criticism and external shame with CFT, though the 2023 meta-analysis flags that evidence for *internal* shame specifically remains "limited and inconsistent," from a small number of low-to-moderate-quality trials — CFT is real and evidence-supported, but not yet at the same evidence tier as, say, CBT for depression.
- **DBT's "opposite action" skill** is specifically taught for shame (shame's action urge is to hide/isolate; opposite action is to disclose to a nonjudgmental other). Effect sizes for opposite-action-based behavioral activation in adjacent anxiety/depression trials run d=0.65–0.85, though this is evidence for the broader technique family, not a shame-specific RCT.
- **Psychodynamic and mentalization-based approaches** for more entrenched, personality-level shame: a 2023 case study (Frontiers in Psychiatry) on narrative-identity development in short-term psychodynamic treatment of avoidant personality disorder, and a 2026 case illustration (Blumenthal, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 2026) on mentalization-based treatment for profound shame states in men with antisocial personality disorder, both show shame as a treatable but often personality-embedded target requiring longer-term work, not just a single-session cognitive reframe.
- **Prevalence/epidemiology:** shame itself is not tracked as a standalone epidemiological variable (it is not a DSM diagnosis), but its clinical proxy — avoidant personality disorder — has an estimated general-population prevalence around 2.4% (consistent across the AVPD/social-anxiety literature cited in the first-pass). This confirms shame-driven presentations are common but usually sub-clinical/subsyndromal rather than meeting a formal AVPD diagnosis, which fits an app that is not a treatment tool.
- **What's in scope for a philosophy-guidance app vs. what needs referral:** ordinary "I feel fundamentally flawed" shame from a life event (a mistake, an exposure, a rejection) is appropriate territory. Shame embedded in AVPD, complex PTSD, or active self-harm/NSSI (noted in the CFT pilot-study literature as a population CFT is used with) is a referral case, not a philosophy-matching case.

## 7. Rejection / heartbreak

**v1.0 mapping:** Isolation, Meaninglessness (unchanged from v0.1 #6)

### Currency check

This category has the single strongest 2024–2026 update found across all five categories in this file:

- **Van der Watt et al., *European Journal of Psychotraumatology*, 2023** (surfaced in 2024–2026 citation activity and directly relevant, so included here despite its nominal 2023 date): a case-case-control study of 2,022 university students found that romantic relationship dissolution (RRD) was associated with **significantly higher PTSD-checklist (PCL-5) scores (mean 41.89) than a DSM-5 Criterion A trauma-event group (34.74) or a non-traumatic-stressor control group (26.86)**, with 72.9% of the RRD group scoring above the clinical PTSD cutoff versus 55.4% of the Criterion-A trauma group. The authors explicitly argue this "challenges the validity of Criterion A as the sole gatekeeper for PTSD diagnosis" and that breakup should be recognized as a potentially traumatic event in its own right. This is a materially new finding not reflected in the first-pass write-up, which cited only the older pain-overlap neuroimaging work (Fisher et al.; Kross et al.).
- **2025 fMRI research** (reported via Frontiers in Psychology mini-review, Aug 2025, and other 2025 summaries) shows breakup-related brain activity in the hippocampus and amygdala resembling patterns seen in trauma and drug withdrawal, and finds childhood-maltreatment history compounds hippocampal-volume effects under breakup stress — extending, not contradicting, the first-pass's reward/craving-circuitry framing (Fisher et al., 2010).
- **New clinical-treatment-model literature**: Ziv-Beiman et al., "An Integrative Model for Treating Romantic Breakup Crises as Traumatic Relational Loss: Synthesizing Grief-Oriented, Trauma-Focused, and Relational Approaches" (*Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 2026), and Farber et al., "After the Thrill Is Gone" (*Psychotherapy*, 2024) — both new since the first-pass, both converge on treating breakup as simultaneously a grief event, a trauma event, and a relational-rupture event, which is a broader clinical framing than the first-pass's single ostracism/reward-circuit lens.

**Net effect on the taxonomy:** no root-mapping change needed (Isolation/Meaninglessness both still fit, arguably better now), but the 2023–2026 literature indicates this category's intensity is sometimes clinically underestimated — "just a breakup" is, per this new data, frequently experienced as comparably or more distressing than a DSM-5 Criterion A trauma. Worth flagging to the philosophy-mapping team: source material should not assume mild/moderate distress by default.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass section did not use the phrase "reasonable inference," but it did draw a conclusion from indirect evidence: that "rejection research... documents identity disruption and loss of an imagined future/life narrative, which is a meaning-structure injury, not just a bond injury" (used to justify the Meaninglessness root). Searching specifically for direct support: the APA Monitor summary and Fisher et al. paper the first-pass cites document reward-circuit and depression-risk findings but do not themselves use "identity disruption" or "narrative loss" language directly. The clearest **direct** support for a meaning/identity-disruption framing of breakup comes from newer sources found here: Slotter, Gardner & Finkel's "self-concept" breakup research (Northwestern, cited widely, examines self-concept content loss after breakup) and the Ziv-Beiman (2026) integrative model, which explicitly frames breakup crises as involving "relational rupture" and requiring "narrative reintegration of self-states" — i.e., **this closes the gap**: the Meaninglessness/identity-disruption root mapping is now directly, not just inferentially, supported.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **No single dominant "breakup protocol"** exists as a named, manualized therapy (unlike CBT-for-depression or PE-for-PTSD), but the literature converges on an eclectic, integrative approach: grief-oriented work (meaning-making, narrative reconstruction) + trauma-informed stabilization (given the PTSD-symptom-level findings above) + relational/attachment work. Farber et al. (2024) found "strong evidence for the value of psychotherapy" generally for post-breakup coping, without pointing to one dominant modality.
- **Pharmacological research is narrow but real**: one trial (PubMed 36031005) tested propranolol-assisted memory reactivation for adjustment disorder following romantic betrayal specifically (not general rejection) — an experimental, not standard-of-care, approach; included here to show the clinical seriousness of this territory, not to suggest an app should touch medication.
- **Prevalence/epidemiology**: breakup/rejection is near-universal as a life event, not a rare clinical presentation — the RRD sample above was 886 of 2,022 unselected university students (i.e., a large fraction of any general population sample will have relevant recent experience). This supports the category's real-world salience as a common, not fringe, presenting complaint.
- **In-scope vs. referral**: ordinary heartbreak/grief processing is squarely appropriate for a philosophy-guidance app. The 72.9%-above-PTSD-cutoff finding is a caution, not a disqualifier — but presentations with flashbacks, dissociation, or functional collapse consistent with clinical PTSD should be flagged toward referral rather than treated as routine "heartbreak" content.

## 8. Feeling stuck / can't decide

**v1.0 mapping:** Freedom (unchanged from v0.1 #7)

### Currency check

**No material updates found that change the core construct or root mapping as of September 2026.** The choice-overload/decision-paralysis literature continued to develop in 2024–2025 but along already-established lines:

- A 2025 hierarchical drift-diffusion-modeling + EEG study ("Mitigating choice overload," *ScienceDirect*, 2025) adds a neural mechanism: reduced attention processing in large choice sets is an early neural marker of cognitive load that precedes a shift toward satisficing — a mechanistic refinement of, not a challenge to, Iyengar & Lepper's original finding.
- Choice-overload meta-analytic work has been extended (a 2025 systematic review in the *Spanish Journal of Marketing* on "maximizing" in consumer choice) but remains centered on consumer-choice contexts, the same domain the first-pass already covered — no new clinical-psychology literature was found that meaningfully extends the Yalom/decisional-avoidance side of this category.
- Analysis-paralysis/decision-paralysis continues to appear mainly in workplace/productivity sources (Cleveland Clinic 2025, Psychology Today 2026, Simply Psychology 2026) rather than peer-reviewed clinical literature — this matches the first-pass's own observation that this territory is clinically thin outside the Yalom/existential-therapy literature.

**Finding: no material updates found as of September 18, 2026** that change this category's name, split status, or root mapping.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass section contains no explicit "reasonable inference" flag — its two central claims (choice-overload mechanism via Iyengar/Lepper/Schwartz; Freedom-root/decisional-avoidance via Yalom's own "problem of choice" framing) are both directly sourced to primary texts. A search for ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) applications to indecision specifically — a natural next question given ACT's relevance to "choosing under uncertainty" — surfaced **no ACT-specific clinical trial targeting chronic indecisiveness as a presenting complaint**; ACT is well-evidenced for anxiety, depression, and chronic pain broadly, but its application to "can't decide" is, at most, a reasonable clinical extrapolation from ACT's general values/committed-action framework, not a directly sourced treatment claim. This is a new gap worth flagging explicitly (the first-pass did not raise it, since it did not discuss treatment approaches for this category at all): **treat "ACT helps with indecision" as an inference from ACT's general mechanism, not a directly sourced finding**, if that claim is used anywhere downstream.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **No single evidence-based "protocol" targets indecisiveness as its own diagnosis** — it is not a DSM/ICD entity. It appears as a *symptom* of major depressive disorder (indecisiveness is a listed diagnostic criterion for MDD in DSM-5), generalized anxiety disorder, and obsessive-compulsive personality traits, and separately as a normal-range personality/temperament variable (trait indecisiveness, chronic procrastination).
- **Prevalence**: chronic procrastination — closely related but not identical to decisional avoidance — is estimated at roughly 20–25% of the general adult population (a fourfold increase from ~5% reported in 1978), per multiple procrastination-prevalence reviews; notably one line of scholarship argues decisional procrastination should not be equated with general (task) procrastination, since "decision avoidance... cannot be compared to general procrastination" — a genuine unresolved definitional debate in the field, not settled science.
- **What's actually used clinically**: when indecisiveness is a *symptom* of MDD/GAD, standard depression/anxiety treatment (CBT, behavioral activation, SSRIs where indicated) is what's used — not indecision-specific treatment. When it's not part of a diagnosable disorder (the more common case, and the one most relevant to this app), it is typically addressed through decision-coaching/choice-architecture techniques (reducing option sets, satisficing heuristics) or through values-clarification work (ACT-adjacent, though see gap-fill above) rather than formal psychotherapy.
- **In-scope vs. referral**: this category is, on the evidence, one of the *least* clinically acute of the five — it rarely on its own indicates a need for referral, unless it's presenting as a symptom cluster within depression (in which case the low mood/anhedonia categories, not this one, should carry the referral signal).

## 9. Fear of failure

**v1.0 mapping:** Freedom, Death (renamed from v0.1 #8 "Fear of failure or of a specific outcome")

### Currency check

**The single most important 2024–2026 development across this entire file concerns this category's Death-root justification**, which the first-pass already flagged as running through the theoretical Terror Management Theory (TMT)/ego-threat bridge rather than direct clinical evidence:

- **Treger, Benau & Timko, *PLoS One*, 2023** (found via 2024–2026-era citation activity; directly load-bearing so included): a pre-registered, well-powered replication attempt (5 studies, 1,255 participants total) of the classic TMT mortality-salience "worldview defense" effect **failed to replicate it** — meta-analyzed effect sizes across the five studies were "not significantly different from zero." The authors are careful to say this may reflect methodological/recruitment factors (online/crowdsourced samples) rather than disproving TMT outright, but they explicitly conclude that "questions persist regarding the reliability of [TMT's] core mortality-salience hypothesis under contemporary research conditions."
- This matters directly for category 9: the first-pass's Death-root justification depends on exactly the mortality-salience mechanism this 2023 replication failure calls into question. **This is a genuine currency-check finding that should soften, not necessarily remove, confidence in the Death root** — TMT as a broad theory still has decades of prior supporting literature, but its flagship experimental paradigm is now under real empirical pressure, and an app citing TMT-based Death-root source material for fear of failure should not present the mortality-salience mechanism as settled science.
- Separately, a 2024 scoping review on "entrepreneurial fear of failure" (35 studies, 2010–2023, PMC) confirms fear of failure remains an active, growing research area but treats it as a self-esteem/opportunity-cost construct, not a death-anxiety construct — consistent with, and reinforcing, the first-pass's own observation that atychiphobia sources "frame it primarily as a self-esteem/shame phobia, not a mortality fear."

**Net effect:** no rename or root-removal recommended (Death stays defensible as a secondary, theory-linked root), but confidence in the TMT bridge specifically should be downgraded from "defensible" to "contested" pending further replication, and this should be flagged clearly to whoever selects Death-root source material for this category.

### Gap-fill

The first-pass explicitly flagged: *"Death is defensible via the TMT/ego-threat bridge but weaker/more indirect than the Freedom link... the guidance layer shouldn't expect atychiphobia sources to talk about mortality directly."* Searching specifically to resolve this: no source was found that directly and empirically connects atychiphobia/fear-of-failure to literal mortality salience (confirming the first-pass's own caution was correct) — and the new 2023 replication-failure finding above (Treger et al.) actively weakens the general mortality-salience mechanism this bridge relies on. **Verdict: remains an inference, and a now-more-uncertain one; do not upgrade to a sourced fact.** Recommend the taxonomy notes make this explicit: Death-root source material for category 9 should lean on impermanence/non-attachment-to-outcome framing (which does not require the mortality-salience mechanism to hold) rather than on TMT's specific "death-thought-accessibility" experimental paradigm.

Additionally, this search newly confirmed (not previously verified in the first-pass) that **"atychiphobia" is not an official DSM-5 diagnosis** — it is popular/clinical shorthand; a person meeting full criteria would be diagnosed under the general "Specific Phobia" category. This is a small but real correction: the first-pass treated atychiphobia as "a specific clinical name... covered by [multiple clinical sources]," which is accurate as description but could be misread as implying formal diagnostic status. Worth noting for accuracy.

### Real-world clinical practice

- **CBT for perfectionism** is the most directly relevant, best-evidenced approach: a meta-analysis of 15 RCTs found CBT-for-perfectionism efficacious with medium-to-large effect sizes on personal standards, concern-over-mistakes, and clinical perfectionism specifically (small-to-medium effects on anxiety, medium on depression/eating-disorder symptoms) — and clinical-perfectionism research explicitly names "fear of failure" and "unrelenting self-criticism" as the core maintaining mechanisms targeted.
- **Exposure therapy** is used when fear of failure crosses into phobic-level avoidance (Cleveland Clinic, Talkspace) — the mechanism is the same as exposure for any specific phobia: graded, repeated engagement with the feared situation until anxiety habituates.
- **ACT and Compassion-Focused Therapy adaptations for perfectionism** were found comparably effective to classical CBT in at least one protocol comparison, with CFT particularly suited to shame-driven perfectionists — a direct clinical bridge between this category and category 6 (Shame) worth noting for the retrieval layer.
- **Prevalence**: atychiphobia/pathological fear-of-failure at phobic intensity is estimated at 2–5% of the population (Cleveland Clinic/Talkspace-sourced figure) — i.e., most people who "fear failure" in the colloquial sense sourced to this category will be in the much larger sub-clinical/perfectionism-spectrum population, not the smaller phobic-level population, which fits an app whose job is philosophy guidance rather than phobia treatment.
- **In-scope vs. referral**: ordinary/sub-clinical fear of failure is squarely in-scope for philosophy guidance. Phobic-level atychiphobia with functional impairment (avoiding job applications, relationships, or health screenings entirely) or fear of failure driving clinical-level depression/panic warrants referral to CBT/exposure-based treatment.

## 10. Substance use

**v1.0 mapping:** Death, Meaninglessness (split from v0.1 #9 "Substance use / compulsive coping"; this file covers only the Substance-use half, per scope)

### Currency check

Several genuine 2024–2026 developments, most of them strengthening the category's clinical grounding without changing its structure:

- **DSM-5-TR Updates (September 2024 and September 2025 supplements, APA)**: over 50 coding updates for substance intoxication/withdrawal and related disorders, effective with ICD-10-CM changes October 1, 2025. These are coding/administrative updates, not conceptual changes — the core substance use disorder (SUD) diagnostic structure (11 criteria; mild/moderate/severe staging) from DSM-5 (2013) is unchanged.
- **Overdose mortality has dropped sharply and recently** — a real, significant epidemiological shift not reflected in the first-pass (which predates this trend becoming clear): CDC provisional data show U.S. overdose deaths falling for three consecutive years — 110,037 (2023) → ~80,000–81,000 (2024, per differing CDC releases) → 69,973 (2025, provisional) — a roughly 27% single-year drop from 2023 to 2024 and continued decline into 2025, with opioid-involved deaths specifically falling from 55,296 (2024) to 44,564 (2025). This is directly relevant to how "real" and current the mortality-risk framing for this category is: overdose remains a leading cause of death in the relevant age ranges, but the trend line has meaningfully improved.
- **2024 NSDUH (National Survey on Drug Use and Health, SAMHSA)**: 16.8% of people 12+ (48.4 million) had a past-year substance use disorder; drug use disorder specifically rose from 8.7% (2021) to 9.8% (2024); of adults with any mental illness, 34.5% also had a co-occurring SUD (47.3% among those with serious mental illness) — confirms very high real-world prevalence and very high psychiatric comorbidity, both consistent with the first-pass's framing.
- **New evidence-based treatment access changes**: SAMHSA guidance (January 2025) raised contingency-management incentive limits from $75 to $750/person/year, and multiple states have Section 1115 waivers in progress (as of March 2025) to fund contingency management — a real, current shift toward evidence-based behavioral incentive treatment as standard of care, not merely mentioned in theory.

### Gap-fill — the Death-root question (flagged priority per task instructions)

The first-pass explicitly flagged: *"Death is more debatable as currently framed—it likely reflects mortality/self-destruction risk (overdose, health decline) rather than a Yalom-style 'impermanence' theme... the literature here doesn't frame substance use primarily as an existential-death-awareness issue, it frames it as an affect-regulation/meaning issue."* This search specifically dug into that flag and found **direct, recent evidence that both confirms and sharpens it**:

- **Kurt, Taşdemir & Gök, *BMC Psychiatry*, 2026** — a case-control study of 86 people with opioid use disorder (in remission) vs. 86 healthy controls found **no statistically significant difference in death anxiety** between groups, but **significantly elevated death-related depression** (a construct defined by hopelessness and meaninglessness rather than fear) in the OUD group, which significantly predicted suicidal ideation (β=0.278, p=0.007), while psychological resilience was protective against both suicidal ideation and impulsivity. The authors' own conclusion: **"existential despair and loss of purpose represent more clinically relevant vulnerability factors than death-based anxiety alone."**
- **A large-sample (N=2,912) Israeli study on symbolic immortality and terror management** found symbolic immortality (belief one will be remembered after death — a TMT "anxiety buffer" construct) was *negatively* associated with problematic substance use and behavioral addictions, but the buffering pathway runs through self-esteem, attachment security, and **meaning in life** — i.e., the protective mechanism against substance use is meaning-related, not fear-of-death-related.

**Verdict: this is no longer just an inference — it is now directly sourced.** The 2026 case-control study is the clearest available evidence that the Death-root connection for substance use runs through **hopelessness/meaninglessness about mortality and loss of purpose (a Meaninglessness-adjacent construct), plus literal physical mortality risk from the substance itself (overdose, organ damage)** — and specifically *not* through Yalom-style death-anxiety/impermanence awareness, which the same study found was not elevated in this population at all. Recommend the taxonomy notes state this plainly: for category 10, Death-root source material should be selected for its resonance with *physical mortality risk and hopelessness*, not for impermanence/awareness-of-death teachings in the Yalom philosophical sense — a meaningfully different recommendation than for category 9 (Fear of failure), where Death-root material was already recommended to lean *away* from literal mortality and toward impermanence. **These two categories need opposite treatment of their shared Death root, and this file makes both directions explicit.**

### Real-world clinical practice

- **Motivational Interviewing (MI)** remains a core, actively-researched, evidence-based approach: 2024–2025 RCTs and real-world studies (Motivational Enhancement for Engagement in Treatment/MEET; MI for comorbid anxiety+SUD showing significant 6-month symptom reductions in both conditions) confirm continued strong evidence, especially for engagement/treatment-motivation and care-transition contexts.
- **Contingency management (CM)** has some of the strongest outcome data of any behavioral SUD intervention: among nearly 3,000 VA patients with stimulant use disorder, CM recipients were 41% less likely to die within a year than non-recipients — a striking mortality-reduction finding, and directly relevant to why this category carries real physiological stakes that a philosophy-guidance app cannot address on its own.
- **Medication-assisted treatment (MAT)** — buprenorphine, methadone, extended-release naltrexone — remains standard of care for opioid use disorder specifically, with buprenorphine associated with up to 3.2x lower overdose-death risk versus no opioid agonist therapy. This is squarely medical treatment territory.
- **CBT relapse-prevention and self-medication-informed psychotherapy**: the Khantzian self-medication framework (carried forward from first-pass) remains actively cited; a 2023 published case report (Abu Al-Khair, *Case Reports in Psychiatry*) documents a 20-year-old man with comorbid social anxiety disorder, depression, and substance abuse treated with 20 sessions of CBT — a genuine example of the self-medication pattern being treated in real clinical practice.
- **Prevalence**: 16.8% past-year SUD prevalence (NSDUH 2024, ages 12+) confirms this is a common, not rare, presenting complaint.
- **In-scope vs. referral — this is the sharpest boundary of any category in this file.** Overdose risk, withdrawal (which can be medically dangerous or fatal for alcohol and benzodiazepines specifically), and high SUD-severity presentations are unambiguous referral/crisis-resource territory, not philosophy-guidance territory — consistent with the taxonomy doc's own exclusion of "acute psychiatric crisis" from v1.0 scope. A philosophy-guidance app should be positioned for the affect-regulation/meaning dimension of substance use (the Khantzian/Frankl material) in people who are not in active withdrawal or acute crisis, with clear, explicit crisis-resource redirection built in given the physiological stakes documented above (overdose mortality, MAT's proven mortality-reduction effect, CM's proven mortality-reduction effect). This is the one category in this file where "what's realistic for a philosophy-guidance app to touch" has a hard, literature-backed ceiling.

### Case grounding

**Real published case (cited, not fabricated).** Abu Al-Khair (2023), *Case Reports in Psychiatry*, "Substance Abuse, Depression, and Social Anxiety: Case Study and Application of Cognitive Psychotherapy" — a 20-year-old male with comorbid social anxiety disorder, depression, and substance abuse, treated across 20 CBT sessions. A genuine, citable published case directly illustrating the self-medication/affect-regulation pattern (Khantzian) this category is grounded in.

**Real published case (cited, not fabricated).** A case report and focused literature review, "Alcohol use disorder with comorbid anxiety disorder" (*Addiction Science & Clinical Practice*, 2022, PMC9644470) — a middle-aged man with anxiety disorder, alcohol use disorder, chronic pain, and GHB use, who improved with naltrexone (targeting AUD), gabapentin (targeting both AUD and anxiety), integrated psychotherapy, and AA/addictions-medicine follow-up. Genuine published case; illustrates both the self-medication pattern and the medical/multi-modal nature of real-world treatment — useful for reviewers judging what's beyond an app's scope.

**Illustrative composite (not a documented case).** A 45-year-old describes drinking nightly "just to turn my brain off," denies any wish to die, but acknowledges not caring much whether they wake up the next day — a presentation consistent with the Kurt et al. (2026) finding that hopelessness/meaninglessness, not death-fear, is the operative existential dimension in substance use, and consistent with Frankl's existential-vacuum framing (carried forward from first-pass). Built from these cited patterns, not from a specific documented case.
